# Build therapy's handoff layer, not Tinder

The app worth building is not a swipe-to-match marketplace for one-off sessions. It is a **therapist-sanctioned coverage and continuity network**: a client's regular therapist hands them to a vetted colleague who is legally licensed where the client is, and the client comes back with a summary. Most of the original concept's headline features already exist or have already failed. **Therapy by Headspace sells $149 pay-as-you-go sessions with no subscription** ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/headspace-launches-insurance-backed-dtc-therapy-offering)), **Grow Therapy had generated more than 1 million AI session summaries for clients by May 2026** ([Grow Therapy](https://growtherapy.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/GrowTherapy_AIResponsibility_WhitePaper_May2026.pdf)), swipe-style therapist apps never gained traction, and travel is the gap scenario with the least evidence behind it. What no platform does is carry a client across organizational lines when care breaks down, whether because the therapist is on leave, the waitlist runs for months, the client is between therapists or home from college, or the client is in a state where their therapist cannot legally practice. That move needs consented context going in and a reviewed summary coming out. The gap rests on hard structure. Licensure follows the client's physical location. Ethics codes oblige therapists to plan for interruptions, which they now handle informally. And **78% of independent clinicians say a trusted peer network makes their practice more sustainable** ([SimplePractice](https://www.simplepractice.com/resource/2026-clinician-well-being-study/)). Revenue should come from therapists (a flat membership) and from clients paying their therapist per session, with universities and employers as later buyers. The reason is survival odds: **consumer-paid digital mental-health companies shut down at a 53% rate versus 21% for institution-paid ones** ([Behavioral Healthcare Network](https://www.bhnet.org/78919/10-mental-health-startups-that-died-from-poor-financial-health/)). No published source answers the questions that decide the outcome: how often gaps happen, whether therapists will cover a colleague's client or see a stranger once, and what anyone will pay. Therapist interviews therefore come before code. A class project can prove the idea with a prototype and interview evidence. A launch needs a single-state pilot that recruits therapists first, built on legal, privacy and crisis-safety work that a class project only has to document.

## Recommendation: a coverage network therapists invite their clients into

The recommendation keeps the original concept's best insight, that the moments between sessions and between therapists are badly served, and drops the parts the evidence says will not work. The product is software that lets solo therapists cover for each other and lets a client in a care gap book one lawful session with a vetted clinician. The client brings their own context into that session, and a summary goes back to their regular therapist afterward. Three connected flows make up the product, and each one answers a documented failure in the current system.

### Three connected flows replace the swipe deck

**Coverage circles** are the therapist-side core. A solo therapist joins a small, vetted group of colleagues. The platform verifies each member's licenses, compact privileges and specialties. Each therapist names who covers their clients during vacation, illness or leave, and records an emergency contact for their professional will. The ethics codes already require this kind of planning. APA Standard 3.12 tells psychologists to plan for services interrupted by "illness, death, unavailability, relocation, or retirement," and NASW 1.15 and AAMFT 1.11 impose parallel duties ([APA Ethics Code](https://www.apa.org/ethics/code/ethics-code-2017.pdf); [NASW Code](https://www.socialworkers.org/About/Ethics/Code-of-Ethics/Code-of-Ethics-English/Social-Workers-Ethical-Responsibilities-to-Clients)). Today's practice is informal: reciprocal swaps with trusted colleagues, a covering clinician's name in an out-of-office message, or staying on call through the holidays ([SimplePractice](https://www.simplepractice.com/blog/how-to-take-vacation/)). When those arrangements fall short, clients can lose care outright. Rula's guidance to therapists says discharging clients "may be appropriate" during extended leave such as maternity leave ([Rula](https://therapistsupport.rula.com/hc/en-us/articles/46641831224603-Taking-time-off-Ethical-and-clinical-best-practices-for-supporting-patients)). APA 10.04 and NASW 3.06 tell clinicians to proceed with caution, and to consult the other provider where appropriate, before treating someone already in care elsewhere. A covering session that starts from the regular therapist's referral meets those standards by design ([APA Ethics Code](https://www.apa.org/ethics/code/ethics-code-2017.pdf); [NASW Code, Section 3](https://www.socialworkers.org/About/Ethics/Code-of-Ethics/Code-of-Ethics-English/Social-Workers-Ethical-Responsibilities-in-Practice-Settings)).

**Lawful bridge sessions** are the client-side core. A client in a gap says what they need today and where they are physically located. They then see only clinicians who are authorized to practice in that location and have posted open telehealth slots that can be booked instantly. Instant booking against posted availability fixes the two failures directory searches are documented to produce. The first is listings that lead nowhere: secret shoppers got appointments from only **18% of Medicare Advantage mental-health listings**, and only **17.8% of listed Medicaid psychiatric prescribers** were reachable and taking new patients ([Senate Finance Committee](https://www.finance.senate.gov/chairmans-news/wyden-calls-for-action-to-get-rid-of-ghost-networks-releases-secret-shopper-study); [JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2821639)). The second is callbacks the therapist controls. In one audit, middle-class white callers got callbacks 51% of the time, against 34% for working-class Black callers ([American Sociological Association](https://www.asanet.org/news_item/study-finds-evidence-racial-and-class-discrimination-among-psychotherapists/)).

The engine underneath bridge sessions is a jurisdiction table. It maps each state against license type, whether a prior relationship exists, and day limits, and it is refreshed as the rules change. It is also the product's most defensible asset, because the rules are fragmented and keep moving. California lets out-of-state therapists see existing clients for 30 days a year. Florida runs an out-of-state registration with no day limit. New York, Texas, Tennessee, Georgia and New Jersey offer no out-of-state behavioral telehealth pathway at all ([CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/); [CA BBS](https://bbs.ca.gov/licensees/temporary_practice_allowance_faqs.pdf)). When the client's own therapist can legally keep seeing them, the app should say so and send the client back. That turns therapists from wary competitors into referrers.

**A context packet and a return summary** connect the two flows. Before a bridge session, the client shares a profile written in their own words: what they are working on, what has and hasn't helped, current medications, their safety plan, their preferences and their regular therapist's contact details. Access expires after the session. Afterward, the covering clinician writes or approves a plain-language summary for the client. A separate clinician-to-clinician note goes to the regular therapist with the client's consent, ideally routed through the client. The UK's Sheffield Mental Health Passport uses the same pattern, a history in the person's "own words" shared through a link they can revoke ([Sheffield Mental Health](https://www.sheffieldmentalhealth.co.uk/mental-health-passports-and-safety-plans-guidance)). The research found no US commercial equivalent. The packet works around a real infrastructure failure. Only **19% of behavioral-health facilities participate in a health information exchange** ([TechTarget on ONC data](https://www.techtarget.com/searchhealthit/feature/ONC-highlights-behavioral-health-EHR-adoption-trends-data-exchange-barriers)), and a formal records request can take 30 days plus a 30-day extension under HIPAA ([45 CFR 164.524](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.524)). A covering clinician seeing someone this week cannot wait for either.

### Solo cash-pay therapists are the first customer; clients in a gap are the first users

Recruit therapists first. The best early supply is solo and small-group private-practice therapists who see at least some cash-pay clients by telehealth. Solo practitioners make up **49.8% of independent clinicians and 85.3% of practices**, and more than 60% of clinicians on SimplePractice can offer an appointment within seven days ([Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/new-report-highlights-independent-mental-health-practice-access-to-care-and-evolving-role-of-ai)). Their top stressor is not documentation but **marketing and client acquisition (48%)**, and **67% say independent practice feels isolating** ([SimplePractice](https://www.simplepractice.com/resource/2026-clinician-well-being-study/)). Telehealth-only practices report median revenue of $70,000, against $93,000 for hybrid practices, which is consistent with underfilled caseloads ([Heard](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice)). Psychologists who hold a PSYPACT E.Passport are the most valuable recruits for cross-state work, because the compact is in effect in about 43 states ([Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)). Counselors are far more limited by state lines: the Counseling Compact is live in only 11 states. Social workers are more limited still, since their compact has issued no multistate licenses ([Counseling Compact](https://counselingcompact.gov/); [Social Work Licensure Compact](https://swcompact.org/)).

On the client side, rank the gap scenarios by how much evidence supports them, not by how vivid they are. **Waitlist bridging** and between-therapist periods come first. **46% of psychologists had no openings for new patients in 2025**, though 87% could see one within three months ([EurekAlert/APA](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1109019)). That leaves a gap of one to three months, which a bridge session fits. **Clients of therapists on extended leave** come next, because those clients are sometimes discharged. **College students home on break** are a seasonal, concentrated group. Campus counselors generally cannot treat students who return to another state, and some schools already pay national telehealth vendors to fill the gap ([Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/colleges-use-teletherapy-to-support-students-outside-of-the-academic-year/625532/)). **Scarce-specialty consults** gain the most from a national pool. These include an ASL-fluent clinician, of whom there are only a handful in many states, or a Spanish-speaking therapist; only **5.5% of psychologists** could provide services in Spanish in 2015 ([NAD](https://www.nad.org/?p=2647); [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2018/06/spanish-speaking)). **Travel** comes last. No source measures how often clients in ongoing therapy travel or what they would pay for a session while away. Incumbents already publish travel policies; Rula, for example, lets sessions continue during domestic trips of under 30 days ([Rula](https://psychsupport.rula.com/hc/en-us/articles/22476941586587-Out-of-state-care)). And a short trip carries little clinical risk for a stable client.

### Therapists pay a flat fee, clients pay per session, institutions pay later

The model has three revenue lines, adopted in order. First, therapists pay a **flat monthly membership**. It covers coverage circles, the context-packet and summary tools, automated cancellation policies and the ability to post open slots. Existing benchmarks bracket the price: Psychology Today charges therapists $29.95 a month for a listing with no booking, and Alma charges $50 a month in 2026 ([eSEOspace](https://eseospace.com/blog/therapy-directories-compared/); [ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/?p=105046)). A flat fee is safer than a per-booking or per-referral fee, because APA 6.07 and NASW 1.16(c) bar clinicians from paying for a referral alone ([APA Ethics Code](https://www.apa.org/ethics/code/ethics-code-2017.pdf); [NASW Code](https://www.socialworkers.org/About/Ethics/Code-of-Ethics/Code-of-Ethics-English/Social-Workers-Ethical-Responsibilities-to-Clients)). Whether a platform booking fee crosses that line is a question for a lawyer.

Second, **clients pay the covering therapist's own posted rate per session**. There is no subscription, and clients get a superbill they can submit for out-of-network reimbursement. The platform takes a small, disclosed processing fee; Grow, for comparison, charges therapists 5% on self-pay sessions ([ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/?p=105046)). Third, once the network has enough therapists in a region, it sells **break and leave coverage to institutions** such as universities, employers and group practices, whose need for coverage recurs predictably.

What the business must not do is buy one-off clients with paid advertising. BetterHelp collects about **$205 per paying user per month** from recurring subscriptions and still ran a **2.2% adjusted EBITDA margin** in Q3 2026 ([Teladoc 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001477449/000147744926000050/tdoc-20260930xexx991.htm)). A one-off session yields one fee for each client acquired. Acquisition has to cost almost nothing, which means it comes from therapists sending their own clients and from institutional contracts. Insurance billing should also wait. Payers have used insurance-billing platforms (Headway, Alma, Grow) to cut what therapists are paid, most recently Aetna's July 2026 move to pay Alma clinicians the same rate for 53-minute sessions as for 45-minute ones ([Behavioral Health Business](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/06/09/aetna-reimbursement-highest-for-psychotherapy-sessions-among-commercial-payers/)).

## Cost and navigation drive unmet need far more than travel does

About half of US adults with a mental illness get no treatment, but most of them are not looking for it. In 2024, **52.1% of the 61.5 million adults with any mental illness received treatment**. Among the 29.5 million who did not, **79% did not perceive a need for care** ([SAMHSA 2024 NSDUH](https://www.samhsa.gov/data/sites/default/files/reports/rpt56287/2024-nsduh-annual-national/2024-nsduh-annual-national-html-071425-edited/2024-nsduh-annual-national.htm)). No matching app reaches that group. The pool a product can address is the roughly 6 million untreated adults who do perceive a need, plus people already in care whose access breaks down. Within that pool, the reasons for not getting care point squarely at navigation and price, not capacity. **65.2% said treatment would cost too much**, **49.2% did not know how or where to get it**, **45.0% could not find the professional they wanted** and **47.9% lacked the time**. Only **16.5% cited a lack of openings** (same source). Those figures argue for a product that makes existing supply easy to find, affordable and quick to book, rather than one that invents new supply.

Directories fail at exactly that job. The secret-shopper studies cited above found that only about 1 in 5 listings leads to an appointment. When a 2020 survey asked directory users about their experience, **53% had hit inaccuracies**, and those who did were four times more likely to receive an out-of-network bill ([APA testimony to Senate Finance](https://www.finance.senate.gov/download/05032023-resnick-testimony&download=1)). Forum accounts describe voicemail boxes that are full, inquiries that go unanswered and the exhaustion of retelling one's story to each new prospect ([Team Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/getting-mental-healththerapists-is-so-freaking-difficult-fttsitjv)). Instant booking against slots a therapist has posted and confirmed is the direct fix, which is why it belongs in the product as plumbing, not as the pitch.

Capacity, meanwhile, is loosening in the part of the market this app would serve. The share of psychologists with no openings fell from **56% in 2023 to 46% in 2025** ([EurekAlert/APA](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1109019)), and SimplePractice reports that most independent clinicians can see someone within a week. The country effectively has two markets. One has hard shortages: rural areas, Medicaid, psychiatry, and the 40% of Americans who live in a mental-health professional shortage area ([HRSA](https://data.hrsa.gov/default/generatehpsaquarterlyreport)). The other is metro and telehealth private practice, where therapists have slack hours and finding clients is their top worry. A bridge-session network can match the slack in the second market to gaps in the first only where licensure allows, which is another reason the jurisdiction engine is central.

### The travel case is legally real but commercially thin

The original concept's travel story rests on a genuine legal wall. Telehealth is legally "rendered at the physical location of the patient" ([CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)). A therapist in Ohio seeing a client who is sitting in Texas is therefore practicing in Texas, and Texas offers no out-of-state pathway. The compacts help less than their membership counts suggest. PSYPACT and the Counseling Compact both require the clinician to be in their own home state during the session, and privileges are acquired one state at a time ([Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)). A traveling client's regular therapist often has no lawful way to see them, and a clinician licensed at the destination does. On that narrow point the concept is right.

The commercial case is thin for three reasons. First, the research found **no data at all** on how often people in ongoing therapy travel, how often sessions are missed because of it, or what they would pay for a session with a stranger while away. Second, many state exceptions apply only to *existing* relationships. Examples include California's 30-day allowance, Washington's continuity-of-care rule, and Illinois's and Wisconsin's limits of 5 days a month and 15 days a year. Those exceptions help the client's own therapist, not a new one-off clinician ([CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)). Third, incumbents already handle the case in their own ways. Rula lets sessions continue during domestic trips of under 30 days. Headway and Spring Health enforce location strictly, and Spring may still charge a late-cancellation fee when a session is blocked by the client's location ([Rula](https://psychsupport.rula.com/hc/en-us/articles/22476941586587-Out-of-state-care); [Headway](https://help.headway.co/hc/en-us/articles/26337138441236-Credentialing-licensing-and-location-requirements); [Spring Health](https://careteam.springhealth.com/hc/en-us/articles/24232935255444-Accessing-Care-While-Out-of-State-or-Traveling)).

Two travel sub-cases do stand out. Foreign visitors physically in a US state can be lawfully served by a therapist licensed there, which makes them an underserved but legal niche. US clients abroad are the opposite. APA advises that the psychologist must be "legally permitted" where the patient is, "including other countries," and US compacts give no cover there ([APA Services](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/legal/technology/telehealth-different-state)). A launch should keep sessions to US locations only.

### AI chatbots now own the "someone, right now" moment

When people cannot get a session, they increasingly turn to a chatbot. **16% of US adults used an AI chatbot for mental-health information in the past year**, rising to 28% of adults under 30 and 30% of the uninsured. About 65% cited speed as a major reason, and **58% did not follow up with a provider** ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/public-opinion/kff-tracking-poll-on-health-information-and-trust-use-of-ai-for-health-information-and-advice/)). Employer assistance programs add free phone counseling around the clock for many workers ([Premera/ComPsych](https://premera.com/documents/017340.pdf)). A paid human session cannot beat free and instant on price or speed, so it should not try. Its edge is human judgment, safety and a handoff to ongoing care.

That edge is real. In an 18-country survey, **45% of people using AI for mental-health advice were dissatisfied with it** ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2026/06/03/more-than-60-people-use-ai-for-mental-health-support-but-many-are-unhappy-with-it-survey-f)). The FDA's Digital Health Advisory Committee recommended human escalation pathways for generative-AI mental-health tools ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/11/FDAs-Digital-Health-Advisory-Committee-Considers-Generative-AI-Therapy-Chatbots-for-Depression)), and state laws in Illinois and Colorado keep licensed clinicians in charge. The defensible tier is "I need a licensed clinician this week, and I am not in crisis": below 988 and the ER, above a chatbot. AI companion products that need somewhere to send users are a plausible distribution partner.

## Incumbents already ship the headline features; what's missing is portability

Every surface feature in the original concept has a well-funded owner. **Pay-per-session therapy** is already offered by Therapy by Headspace at $149, by general telehealth providers at roughly $100 to $284, and by Grow Therapy with insurance ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/digital-health/headspace-launches-insurance-backed-dtc-therapy-offering)). **Near-same-day booking** exists too. Zocdoc says "same-day appointments are often available" for psychotherapists who take a client's insurance ([Zocdoc](https://www.zocdoc.com/doctors/licensed-clinical-psychologists)), and Grow offers a filter for availability within 72 hours. One vendor warns that "same-day" usually means "within 24 hours," and that a slot can slip if the therapist must accept the request first ([Klarity](https://helloklarity.com/post/same-day-therapist-appointment-online-2026-guide)). No usage data exists for any of these offerings, so it is unknown whether they succeed. They do mean a no-commitment one-off session is not new.

**Therapist-to-client session summaries** are now standard in therapy software. Grow sends AI "session recaps" to consenting clients about 15 to 20 minutes after a session. Until a November 10, 2026 change added a per-client "Review first" option, therapists could not review or edit a recap before it went out ([Grow Therapy](https://help.growtherapy.com/en/articles/13873367-grow-ai-summarization-session-insights)). SimplePractice launched "Client Summaries" in June 2026 ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260617564693/en/)). Upheal's client portal shows shared summaries alongside booking and forms, and has done since March 2026 ([Upheal](https://feedback.upheal.io/changelog/your-clients-now-have-their-own-upheal-portal-fire)). Talkspace sends clients a three-to-five-minute AI podcast after each session. It reports that members who open one between their first and second sessions are 20% more likely to complete a second and third session, a company figure ([Quartr](https://quartr.com/events/talkspace-talk-q1-2026_ojV8RZXm)). **Journaling** is bundled into BetterHelp alongside worksheets and a habit tracker ([BetterHelp](https://www.betterhelp.com/advice/psychology/how-much-does-betterhelp-cost/)). A handful of small products let clients share journal entries with a therapist. Their designs diverge: SafeTalk lets the client reveal individual entries, while Therachat deliberately shares only the *date and time* of entries "to respect the boundaries of the therapy relationship" ([SafeTalk](https://safetalk.space/blog/help-centre/how-safetalk-journaling-works-a-therapists-perspective/); [Therachat](https://therachat.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/151000119081-journal-activity-)).

### Swipe matching was never the bottleneck

No swipe-style therapist app has verifiable US traction. MindSwipe and "Cocoon: Find Your Therapist" exist as App Store listings, and Cocoon showed three reviews ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/cocoon-v2/id6479022643)). The closest serious attempt, Mental Health Match, was founded after its creator watched a friend use a dating app. By 2021 it had **2,000 therapists listed but only 119 paying customers** ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2021/mental-health-match-helps-people-find-right-therapist-finds-new-home-portland/)). Therapist marketplaces in Poland and Lithuania closed because they "never reached enough volume" ([Behavioral Healthcare Network](https://www.bhnet.org/78919/10-mental-health-startups-that-died-from-poor-financial-health/)). A design case study found the swipe gesture is not intuitive for people who don't use dating apps ([Medium](https://medium.com/@jordanleslie/the-dating-app-to-help-you-find-your-next-therapist-a79502c35a19)). Tinder itself moved toward AI recommendations in 2025 to reduce "swipe fatigue" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3089496)).

The matchers that scaled, among them BetterHelp, Headway, Grow, Alma and Rula, won on payment and supply: subscriptions or insurance billing, credentialing, and caseloads filled for therapists. None of them won on interface. A swipe deck laid over thin, location-restricted supply mostly shows clients how scarce that supply is. The "Tinder" label also carries clinical baggage, since the ethics literature treats therapists and clients on dating apps as a boundary problem ([Springer, Contemporary Family Therapy](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10591-020-09561-7)). It works as pitch shorthand inside a class or a team, but not as a brand that therapists, payers or regulators will see.

### Portability across organizations is the open gap

What incumbents do not do is move context *between* organizations. Talkspace lets a switching client choose to share chat history with the new therapist, but only inside Talkspace ([Talkspace](https://help.talkspace.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000287406)). Spring Health bought Alma to solve what it calls "care continuity," the "third and hardest barrier." Its version keeps members with their providers through job and coverage changes inside its own network of 40,000-plus providers ([Spring Health](https://www.springhealth.com/blog/why-spring-health-acquired-alma)). Each therapy software portal is a silo owned by one practice, so a client who sees a covering therapist gets another login and no shared history.

The public plumbing does not fill the gap either. Behavioral health was left out of the 2009 HITECH electronic-records incentives. Many private-practice therapy software products are not federally certified and so lack the mandated patient-access interfaces. HIPAA "psychotherapy notes" are excluded from the information-blocking rules entirely ([MACPAC](https://macpac.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Integrating-Clinical-Care-through-Greater-Use-of-Electronic-Health-Records-by-Behavioral-Health-Providers.pdf); [ASTP/ONC](https://healthit.gov/faq/does-electronic-health-information-definitions-exclusion-psychotherapy-notes-apply-notes/)). A record the client writes and controls avoids all three problems, because the client is the source and no records release is needed. That is why the context packet, not the summary feature, is the differentiator worth building.

## One-off consumer sessions are the weakest business model in the sector

The market has already run the experiment of consumer-paid therapy, and the results point one way. Every major direct-to-consumer therapy brand that survived has moved toward payers. BetterHelp's paying users fell to an average of **336,000 in Q3 2026, down 12%**, and its cash-pay revenue fell **25%**. Revenue from insurance-covered sessions rose from $3.7 million to $32.1 million over the same year ([Teladoc 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001477449/000147744926000050/tdoc-20260930xexx991.htm)). At Talkspace, consumer subscriptions had shrunk to **$17.5 million of $228.9 million in 2025 revenue (about 7.6%)**, while payor revenue grew 37.9% ([Talkspace 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1803901/000119312526058146/talk-ex99_1.htm)). Universal Health Services then bought Talkspace for about **$835 million**, against a SPAC valuation of roughly $1.4 billion five years earlier ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/hospital-transactions-and-valuation/uhs-closes-835m-talkspace-acquisition-creating-end-to-end-behavioral-health-continuum/); [MedCity News](https://medcitynews.com/2021/11/talkspace-leaders-depart-as-companys-growth-falls-short-of-expectations/)).

Survival data across the sector says the same. In a dataset of 542 digital mental-health organizations, **consumer-paid companies shut down or went bankrupt 53% of the time, versus 21% where an institution paid**, and **one-time-purchase businesses had 85% mortality** ([Behavioral Healthcare Network](https://www.bhnet.org/78919/10-mental-health-startups-that-died-from-poor-financial-health/)). The dataset's methodology is not published, so treat the figures as directional. They are still a warning to any model built on single, consumer-paid transactions.

On-demand healthcare is a graveyard for the same structural reason: demand per visit is unpredictable while idle capacity costs money. Amazon Care closed at the end of 2022, saying it was "not a complete enough offering" for enterprise customers ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/amazon-care-shutting-down-end-2022-tech-giant-said-virtual-primary-care-business-wasnt)). Walmart Health closed all 51 of its health centers in 2024, citing a "challenging reimbursement environment" ([Healthcare Brew](https://www.healthcare-brew.com/stories/2024/04/30/walmart-to-close-its-healthcare-clinics)). Forward shut abruptly after raising about $400 million, leaving patients scrambling for their records ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/primary-care-player-forward-shutters-after-raising-400m-rolling-out-carepods); [Becker's ASC](https://www.beckersasc.com/asc-news/patients-scramble-to-retrieve-records-after-startup-abruptly-closes/)).

The on-demand models that worked had an institution paying for access. In 2020, **79% of Teladoc's revenue came from subscription access fees** and only 19% from visit fees ([Teladoc 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477449/000155837021002118/tdoc-20201231x10k.htm)). Ginger's 24/7 "on-demand" layer was text coaching sold through employers and health plans, with scheduled licensed therapy behind it ([InsuranceNewsNet](https://insurancenewsnet.com/oarticle/ginger-announces-35-million-in-series-c-funding-to-accelerate-global-access-to-on-demand-behavioral-healthcare-through-employers-and-health-plans)). Coaches can be pooled nationally. Licensed therapists cannot, because each state's pool must be staffed separately, and that multiplies idle capacity.

### The unit economics close only if acquisition is nearly free

The arithmetic is unforgiving. BetterHelp earns about $205 a month from each paying user on a recurring subscription, yet its adjusted EBITDA margin was 2.2% in Q3 2026 after advertising. A one-off cash session at $100 to $200 brings in roughly one month of BetterHelp-level revenue per client acquired, once, and the platform keeps only a small fee on it. Paid acquisition for one-off clients therefore cannot work. The research found no credible published acquisition-cost figure for any therapy marketplace, but the margin math does not need one.

The design responds in three ways. Therapists pay a recurring membership, so platform revenue does not depend on session volume. Clients arrive through their own therapist's referral, which costs the platform nothing. And institutions buy coverage in bulk, so recurring need meets a recurring buyer. This is the gig-nursing lesson applied to therapy. Nurse marketplaces such as Clipboard Health and ShiftKey reached valuations above $1 billion because facilities need shifts filled every week ([Truthout/The Markup](https://truthout.org/articles/how-does-gig-nursing-work-affect-patients-nurses-and-health-care-facilities)). A traveler needing therapy once a year is the opposite kind of buyer.

Capital markets reinforce the point. Mental health was the top-funded clinical area for the seventh straight year in the first half of 2026. The money concentrated in a few large rounds for payer-funded, recurring-care models, such as Talkiatry's $210 million and Grow's $150 million at a $3 billion valuation ([Rock Health](https://rockhealth.com/insights/h1-2026-funding-and-market-overview-durable-roots-shifting-routes/); [Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/grow-therapy-raises-150m-series-d-20260305)). A cash-pay network built around care gaps is a contrarian bet. It should plan for slower, institution-led growth rather than a venture-scale consumer launch.

### Therapists have open hours but deep distrust of intermediaries

Supply is available, but it comes with conditions. Private-practice therapists set cash fees of **$130 to $185**, while insurance pays them **$95 to $125**, and 25.4% of practices are cash-only ([Heard](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice)). Platforms pay less again. Therapists' self-reported medians for a 60-minute insured session (billing code 90837) are **$120 on Headway, $110 on Alma, $88 on Grow and $80 on Rula** ([ChoosingTherapy](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/?p=105046)). Talkspace pays contractors **$70 to $90** per 60-minute live session ([Talkspace](https://talkspace.com/join-our-network/therapist)). BetterHelp's pay scale has been reported as starting at $30 an hour ([Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.com/Reviews/Employee-Review-BetterHelp-E1304061-RVW90184722.htm)). Any bridge-session network has to pay therapists at least the cash-market rate after fees. The simplest way to guarantee that is to let them set their own price.

Distrust is specific and earned. Payers have pushed independent therapists onto insurance-billing platforms with the promise of higher rates, and payers' venture arms have invested in those platforms: Cigna Ventures and Optum Ventures in Alma, HCSC in Headway. Payers then cut rates through them ([Behavioral Health Business](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/06/09/aetna-reimbursement-highest-for-psychotherapy-sessions-among-commercial-payers/)). Optum-driven cuts on Alma and Headway in late 2024 cost therapists an estimated $6,000 to $28,000 a year ([ClearHealthCosts](https://clearhealthcosts.com/blog/2024/11/2-digital-mental-health-platforms-cut-pay-rates-for-therapists-with-unitedhealths-optum-stirring-anger/)). Aetna's 2026 rate equalization for Alma clinicians set off an r/therapists megathread whose top comment estimated a roughly 40% pay cut for psychologists ([r/therapists snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1tj1bl1/snapshots/2026-05-22T06%3A54%3A50.751336Z)). Leaving a platform can mean losing in-network status, and BetterHelp's $7.8 million FTC settlement over sharing health data with advertisers still colors how therapists see apps ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook)). Therapists in 2026 will read any new intermediary as a future rate-cutter. The product has to stay out of the payer role, keep clients and credentials with the therapist, and promise in writing never to lock them in or sell their data.

### Filling cancelled slots is weaker than it looks

The supply idea that seems most obvious, filling no-show slots with one-off clients, has problems. Published missed-appointment rates run about **15% of scheduled psychotherapy sessions** and **22% to 40% of first appointments** ([DeFife et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221688088_Psychotherapy_appointment_no-shows_Rates_and_reasons); [Current Psychiatry](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/013_0914CP_Gajwani_FINAL.pdf)). At the median solo caseload of 18 clients a week, that implies roughly two to three empty slots a week. That is an estimate, not a sourced figure.

Most of those slots are poor candidates. A no-show becomes known only when the session is due to start, which is too late to rebook. Filling a late-cancelled slot often replaces the cancellation fee instead of adding to it. Many therapists use found time to catch up on notes. And a new one-off client brings a full intake, a risk screen and a note for one session's revenue. The better fit is late cancellations made a few hours ahead, for cash-pay telehealth, with the client's intake already completed on the platform. No study has measured whether therapists would fill such slots with strangers.

The cancellation problem points to a different product. Therapists describe enforcing late-cancel fees as emotionally costly ("I hate enforcing it and almost always find a way to give my clients an out") ([Helpsquad, quoting r/therapists](https://helpsquad.com/blog/therapy-cancellation-policy-a-free-template-and-how-to-enforce-it/)). That makes automated policy enforcement worth something on its own: card on file, automatic fee, and an automatic waiver if the slot is filled. This kind of single-user utility keeps therapists paying a membership before the network has any marketplace demand.

### Coverage helps clients but does not fix therapists' income problem

One honest limit belongs in the pitch. **68% of burned-out clinicians say they cannot take time off or sick leave without losing income** ([SimplePractice](https://www.simplepractice.com/resource/2026-clinician-well-being-study/)). Coverage circles do not solve this, because the covering clinician earns the fee. Sharing that revenue back to the absent therapist would likely violate APA 6.07 and NASW 1.16(c), unless the payment is tied to real services such as preparing the handoff note. Counsel should design that structure. What coverage circles do offer is peace of mind, compliance with ethics-code duties, and clients who are kept rather than discharged. The gig-nursing record shows what to avoid: shift bidding at the lowest wage, opaque algorithmic allocation, deactivations without due process and unpaid cancelled shifts. Those practices drew Department of Labor misclassification cases and worker backlash ([Roosevelt Institute](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/uber-for-nursing/)). Therapists should post the slots they already have, set their own rates and control their own calendars.

## One session helps briefly, so design every session as a bridge

The clinical case for single sessions is real but smaller than the headlines suggest. The 2025 umbrella review covered 24 systematic reviews of 415 trials and about 40,600 participants. **83% of the reviews reported positive effects** on at least one outcome, with a pooled standardized mean difference of about −0.25 against controls ([Northwestern](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2025/02/single-session-interventions-significantly-reduce-mental-health-issues-for-youth-and-adults)). In the founding youth meta-analysis, though, effects were 0.41 against no-treatment controls but only 0.14 against active controls, and the effect on depression was not significant ([PMC11420571](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11420571/); [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/41292903)).

The adult evidence is weaker still. A 2026 preregistered megastudy tested 12 digital single-session interventions in **7,505 US adults** with elevated depressive symptoms. Nearly all of them helped immediately after the session, with effects up to d = 0.37. By four weeks only two beat the control condition, at d = 0.14 to 0.15, and neither survived correction for multiple comparisons. The sessions also left participants slightly less ready to change, which the authors flag as a possible iatrogenic effect. They conclude that single-session interventions "are not a replacement for psychotherapy" ([Schleider Lab megastudy](https://www.schleiderlab.org/uploads/2/1/8/4/21847128/a_crowdsourced_megastudy_of_12_digital_single-session_interventions_for_depression_in_us_adults.pdf)). A separate preregistered adult trial with 828 participants found no meaningful benefit at two or eight weeks ([JMIR 2023](https://jmir.org/2023/1/e45411)).

Human-delivered walk-in therapy in Ontario got clients feeling better faster than a waitlist did at four weeks. By ten weeks the two groups were indistinguishable, and one cost-effectiveness analysis found walk-in care not cost-effective against the waitlist ([WLU Scholars](https://scholars.wlu.ca/scwk_faculty/16); [Journal of Mental Health](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09638237.2017.1340619)). Most of this evidence comes from digital, self-guided sessions, not from a one-off with a human clinician. **No trial has tested the original concept's core case**: a single session with an unfamiliar therapist for someone already in ongoing care.

Single sessions are also how much real therapy already ends. In US household survey data, **28% of adult psychotherapy users stopped after one or two visits in 2021** ([ScienceDaily on Olfson et al.](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/12/241204113537.htm)). Taken together, the evidence supports a one-off session as a **bridge with a defined next step**, not a fix. Each session should open with a goal for the session ("what do you need today"), end with a written plan, and trigger a check-in at two to four weeks, when the immediate gains have usually faded. Routine outcome feedback adds a small, reliable benefit (d ≈ 0.15), largest for clients who are not on track ([de Jong et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11703940)). That makes a two-to-four-week PHQ-9 and GAD-7 follow-up worth automating.

### Matching works on measured outcomes, not on profiles

The only randomized trial of a matching method did not match on photos, bios or demographics. Constantino and colleagues rated each of 48 therapists, using at least 15 past cases each, as effective, neutral or ineffective in 12 problem areas. Patients were matched to therapists strong in their most elevated areas, and the comparison group was assigned as usual. Matched patients improved faster, with effects of **d = 0.75 for general impairment and d = 0.50 for global distress** ([UMass](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/evidence-based-patient-psychotherapist-matching-improves-mental-health-care)). The working alliance predicts outcome robustly (r = .278 across 295 studies), and it holds online ([Flückiger et al.](https://www.societyforpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Fluckiger-et-al-2018.pdf)). Accommodating client preferences reduces dropout (odds ratio 1.79) and modestly improves outcomes (d = 0.28) ([Swift et al. 2018](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2086257428)).

Racial and ethnic matching shows a split result. Clients strongly prefer it (d = 0.63) and rate matched therapists more favorably, but it has almost no effect on outcomes (d = 0.09) ([Cabral & Smith](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/80)). Therapists explain about **5% of outcome variance** on average, but up to 29% in some settings ([Johns et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/138182/1/Johns%20et%20al.%20A%20systematic%20review%20of%20therapist%20effects%20-%20author%27s%20copy%20311018.pdf)).

These findings translate into a specific matching design. Start with an intake that asks what the client needs today. Filter for clinicians lawfully allowed to practice where the client is and available now. Offer identity, language and specialty filters as client preferences, and don't claim they improve outcomes. Log outcomes by therapist and by problem area from the first session onward, so the platform can earn performance-based matching later. It cannot start there: like Constantino's trial, it needs about 15 cases per therapist first. In the meantime, steering clients away from consistently ineffective therapists will add more value than fine-tuning matches.

### Continuity risk concentrates in high-risk clients, so screen every session

For stable outpatients, an occasional session with a different clinician is unlikely to cause harm. A 2026 Dutch trial randomized 163 depressed patients who had not responded to treatment to stay with their therapist, switch therapist, or switch both therapist and method. It found no differences in outcome ([HCPLive](https://www.hcplive.com/view/switching-therapists-psychotherapy-offers-no-advantage-mdd)). Video therapy for depression matches in-person therapy almost exactly (g = 0.04) ([PubMed 35007437](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35007437/)).

Fragmented care is a different matter for high-risk clients. One estimate attributes **about 23% of suicides partly to continuity failures**, such as abrupt termination of services or the loss of a case manager ([Frontiers in Public Health](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1266717/full)). Risk is also common at first contact. A university walk-in clinic flagged suicidality in roughly 10% to 13% of first appointments, by calculation from its reported counts ([PMC11142450](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11142450/)). Safety-planning interventions cut subsequent suicidal behavior (relative risk 0.57; 16 people treated per event prevented) ([Nuij et al.](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354)). The best-known result paired the safety plan with at least two follow-up calls, and in that study suicidal behavior fell to 3.03% from 5.29% ([Healio on Stanley et al.](https://www.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20180711/intervention-with-followup-reduces-suicidal-behavior-after-ed-discharge)).

A strictly one-off model leaves out exactly that follow-up. Every bridge session therefore needs a structured risk screen beforehand, such as PHQ-9 item 9, which separates one-year suicide-attempt risk of 0.4% from risk of 4% ([Psychiatric Services](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ps.201200587)). It also needs a safety-plan protocol in the session, an emergency pathway tied to the client's actual location, and an automatic follow-up within about seven days for anyone who screens positive. Acute-risk clients go to 988 or emergency services, not to a bridge session, and the app should never market itself for crises.

### Shared summaries help; shared notes and monitored journals create problems

Patients mostly benefit from reading their mental-health notes. Among patients with serious mental illness who read their notes, 20% said doing so made them more likely to take their medications, against 14% of patients with no mental-health diagnosis. Those with other mental-health diagnoses were slightly more likely to feel worried (5% versus 3%) ([JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2777857)). Veterans trusted clinicians more when notes matched the session and recorded their strengths, and less when notes contained errors or stale copied text ([VA Research](https://www.research.va.gov/currents/0217-8.cfm)). The downside is real, though. A 2025 review found that open access makes notes simpler and changes their tone, and warns that it can "compromise the integrity of documentation by omitting sensitive findings and expert judgment" ([JMIR 2025](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e64762)).

The answer is two layers. The client gets a summary written for the client. Clinical impressions travel separately, clinician to clinician, with the client's consent, and the client knows both exist. Grow's November 2026 shift toward letting therapists review recaps before they go out points the same way ([Grow Therapy](https://help.growtherapy.com/en/articles/13873367-grow-ai-summarization-session-insights)).

Journals deserve more caution than the original concept gave them. Expressive writing has a small average effect (about d = 0.15), and a 39-study meta-analysis found no benefit from brief, self-directed writing for depression ([Reinhold et al.](https://paul-buerkner.github.io/publications/pdf/2018__Reinhold_et_al__Clinical_Psychology_Science_and_Practice.pdf)). Mental-health apps keep only **3.3% of users at 30 days** ([JMIR 2019](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/E14567/)), so a product built around daily journaling builds on sand. A therapist who *can* read a journal also risks being expected to read it. In one malpractice case study, a counselor received frequent after-hours texts from a client who later died by suicide; the mother demanded a $500,000 settlement, and the licensing board reviewed the message history ([HPSO](https://www.hpso.com/Resources/Legal-and-Ethical-Issues/Counselor-Case-Study-Inappropriate-dual-relationship-Failure-to-assign)). For a covering clinician with no ongoing relationship, a live journal feed is especially hazardous. Journals should therefore work on a push model: the client picks excerpts to share for a specific session, access expires afterward, and a "not monitored" notice and crisis resources appear in the journal itself.

## Licensure, privacy and AI law make compliance part of the product

For this app, the legal layer is not overhead to clear before launch. Much of it *is* the product, because lawful routing is the service clients cannot get elsewhere.

### The client's location at each session decides who may treat them

The client's physical location has to be captured and verified **at the start of every session**, not once at signup. A traveling or relocating client's state changes, and with it the set of clinicians who can lawfully see them. NASW's technology standards already require social workers to verify a client's identity *and location* for electronic services, and to comply with the laws "in the jurisdiction in which the client is located" ([NASW Code](https://www.socialworkers.org/About/Ethics/Code-of-Ethics/Code-of-Ethics-English/Social-Workers-Ethical-Responsibilities-to-Clients)).

The rules the engine must encode are detailed and keep changing. West Virginia's interstate registration bars registrants from establishing *new* client relationships. Vermont's telehealth registration caps practitioners at 10 unique patients over 120 days. Connecticut's out-of-state pathway expired in June 2025, and Indiana's ended in 2024 ([CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)). Registration states widen the supply of clinicians who can take *new* one-off clients. Florida is the standout: a major travel destination with an uncapped registration that had about **5,573 out-of-state counselors and 2,728 out-of-state psychologists** registered by early 2025, according to an advocacy group ([Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/)).

Duties also follow the client's location. **29 states impose a mandatory duty to warn or protect, 16 make it permissive and 3 have no provision** ([NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/health/mental-health-professionals-duty-to-warn)). The app should show each therapist the regime that applies in the client's state before the session starts. Prescribing and Medicare billing should stay out of the first version. The DEA's telemedicine flexibilities for controlled substances run only through December 31, 2026, and Medicare's in-person requirement for behavioral-health telehealth returns in January 2028 ([McDermott+](https://www.mcdermottplus.com/insights/dea-extends-telemedicine-flexibilities-for-controlled-substance-prescribing-for-2026/); [Medical Economics](https://medicaleconomics.com/view/what-changed-in-medicare-telehealth-under-the-2026-spending-bill)).

### The platform is both a HIPAA business associate and an FTC-regulated health app

The app will almost certainly sit under two privacy regimes at once. When it hosts video, summaries and records for therapists who are HIPAA covered entities, it acts as their business associate and needs agreements with them. When it holds data the client keeps for themselves, such as a private journal or the context packet before it is shared, consumer-protection law applies. The FTC's 2024 update to the Health Breach Notification Rule explicitly covers health apps outside HIPAA. It counts *unauthorized disclosure*, not just hacking, as a breach, and it requires notice within 60 days ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule)). Washington's My Health My Data Act reaches inferences about mental health drawn from non-health data, applies to companies outside the state that target Washington consumers, and gives consumers a **private right of action** ([Washington AG](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy)).

The most-penalized pattern in this sector is ad-tech sharing. BetterHelp paid $7.8 million after the FTC alleged it shared intake answers with Facebook, Snapchat, Criteo and Pinterest. Cerebral shared data on about **3.2 million users** with advertising platforms through tracking tools and is now permanently barred from using health data for ads ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2024/06/11/cerebral-settles-ftc-charges-over-data-privacy-and-deceptive-practices)). The FTC treats even browsing and location data that support a health inference as health information ([FTC guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). Which therapist a user viewed counts.

The two-layer handoff also fits HIPAA's categories. "Psychotherapy notes" under 45 CFR 164.501 are process notes kept separate from the rest of the record. The definition explicitly excludes any summary of diagnosis, treatment plan, symptoms or progress ([45 CFR 164.501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.501)). A client summary is therefore part of the regular record the client already has a right to see. Sharing it is legally low-friction; the risk lies in what it says.

Journal entries change status once a therapist uses them. Entries a therapist reads and relies on in treatment likely become part of the clinical record, which means they are accessible to the client, subject to retention rules and discoverable in litigation. Substance-use confidentiality rules (42 CFR Part 2) can be kept out of scope by not marketing addiction treatment. Minors' consent rules vary too much by state for a first version, so launch for adults only.

### AI must stay administrative, reviewed and consented

State law now draws a clear line. Illinois bars AI from providing therapy or making therapeutic decisions, while allowing administrative and supplementary support ([IDFPR](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/news/2025/gov-pritzker-signs-state-leg-prohibiting-ai-therapy-in-il.html)). In 2026, Colorado, Maine, Vermont and Rhode Island followed with similar limits. Colorado, Maine and Rhode Island require **written notice and consent before AI records or transcribes a session**, and Maine also requires disclosure of whether session data trains models ([Manatt Health AI Policy Tracker](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)). A proposed class action alleges that Sharp HealthCare used an ambient AI scribe to record about 100,000 encounters without valid consent. The claims are unproven, but the plaintiff found out by reading his own visit note ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/healthcare-information-technology/ai/patient-sues-sharp-healthcare-over-ambient-ai-use/)).

Clinicians share the worry. **60% of psychologists are concerned about AI hallucinations** ([EurekAlert/APA](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1109019)), and therapists have reported AI notes that invented suicidal ideation or trauma histories the client never mentioned ([KevinMD](https://kevinmd.com/2026/03/the-hidden-risks-of-ai-generated-progress-notes-in-psychotherapy.html)). Standalone AI therapy is fragile as a business too. Woebot retired its consumer app largely because of "the cost and challenge" of meeting FDA requirements ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2025/07/02/woebot-mental-health-chatbot-shuts-down-founder-says-ai-moving-faster-than-regulators/)).

The safe scope is narrow. AI can draft intake summaries and post-session summaries that a therapist reviews and approves. Recording requires state-aware written consent and must always have a no-recording alternative. Session data is never used for training by default. The product has no client-facing therapeutic chatbot and runs no AI mood or risk analysis on journals. Automated risk detection could trip Illinois-style limits and create a duty to act on what it finds.

### Connect clients with clinicians; don't practice therapy

The platform should present itself as connecting clients with licensed, independent therapists, never as providing therapy. Vermont's 2026 law bars entities from offering mental-health services unless a licensed professional provides them. Pennsylvania sued Character Technologies in May 2026 under its Medical Practice Act over a chatbot that presented itself as a psychiatrist ([Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)). The risk of being treated as practicing therapy rises if the platform sets treatment protocols, controls clinical content or pays clinicians by volume.

Done Global is the extreme case. It paid clinicians for processing prescription refills, some renewals took under a minute, and its founder was convicted in 2025 ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/founder-telehealth-startup-done-convicted-adderall-fraud-scheme); [Wikipedia, citing DOJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Done_Global)). A slot-filling marketplace carries a milder version of the same temptation: rewarding speed and volume over judgment.

### Design rules the evidence supports

| Design rule | Evidence behind it |
|---|---|
| Capture identity and physical location at the start of every session; match only clinicians lawfully allowed to practice there; tell clients when their own therapist can legally continue | Telehealth occurs where the patient is; exceptions vary by state and relationship ([CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)) |
| Run a structured risk screen before every bridge session, route acute risk to 988 or emergency services, and follow up within about 7 days on any positive screen | 10–13% suicidality flags at first contact; safety plans work best with follow-up ([PMC11142450](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11142450/); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20180711/intervention-with-followup-reduces-suicidal-behavior-after-ed-discharge)) |
| Frame each session as a bridge: a goal at the start, a written plan at the end, a PHQ-9/GAD-7 check-in at 2–4 weeks | Single-session gains fade by 4–10 weeks ([megastudy](https://www.schleiderlab.org/uploads/2/1/8/4/21847128/a_crowdsourced_megastudy_of_12_digital_single-session_interventions_for_depression_in_us_adults.pdf)) |
| Start from the regular therapist's referral where one exists; record the client's acknowledgment of existing care and consent to coordinate | APA 10.04 and NASW 3.06 ([APA Ethics Code](https://www.apa.org/ethics/code/ethics-code-2017.pdf)) |
| Share client summaries the therapist has reviewed; never share process notes; send a separate clinical handoff note with consent | Documentation degrades under open access ([JMIR 2025](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e64762)); [45 CFR 164.501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.501) |
| Make journals client-pushed and specific to a session, with access that expires, a "not monitored" notice and crisis resources on screen | Monitoring liability ([HPSO](https://www.hpso.com/Resources/Legal-and-Ethical-Issues/Counselor-Case-Study-Inappropriate-dual-relationship-Failure-to-assign)); low retention ([JMIR 2019](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/E14567/)) |
| No third-party ad pixels, SDKs or retargeting on any screen touching intake, matching or booking | BetterHelp and Cerebral FTC orders ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook)) |
| AI only drafts; a therapist reviews everything; written consent before recording; no training on session data; a no-recording path | 2026 state AI laws ([Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)) |
| Therapists set their own prices and calendars; no lowest-bid auctions, no ranking by speed or volume, no deactivation without due process | Gig-nursing backlash ([Roosevelt Institute](https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/uber-for-nursing/)); Done's per-refill pay |
| No lock-in or non-circumvention clauses; clients who convert to ongoing care belong to the therapist | Platform distrust and payer capture ([Behavioral Health Business](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/06/09/aetna-reimbursement-highest-for-psychotherapy-sessions-among-commercial-payers/)) |
| Per-session client pricing with no auto-renewal; one-click cancellation for therapist memberships | Cerebral's cancellation order; billing is the top complaint category ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/cerebral-agrees-7m-settlement-ftc-doj-over-user-data-sharing-cancellation-practices)) |
| Charge a flat membership rather than per-referral fees; get a legal opinion on any booking fee | APA 6.07; NASW 1.16(c) |
| Launch for adults only and US locations only, with no prescribing and no Medicare billing | Minors' consent varies by state; foreign law applies to clients abroad; DEA and Medicare deadlines |
| Build records export and a wind-down plan from day one | Forward's patients scrambled for records ([Becker's ASC](https://www.beckersasc.com/asc-news/patients-scramble-to-retrieve-records-after-startup-abruptly-closes/)) |
| Say "connect with a licensed therapist," never "we provide therapy" | Vermont's 2026 law; Pennsylvania v. Character Technologies |

## Seven risks, ranked by how likely each is to kill the idea

**First, demand is unmeasured.** Not one source the research found quantifies how often clients in ongoing therapy hit a gap: therapist away, client traveling, between therapists, or waitlisted while already in care. Nor does any source measure what clients would pay for a session with a clinician they don't know. The gap scenarios are structurally real, but the core use case could turn out to be a feature rather than a business. The product would then be stuck between free alternatives (chatbots, EAP phone lines, informal continuation by the client's own therapist) and clients' reluctance to pay $150 for one conversation with a stranger.

**Second, therapists' willingness is unmeasured.** No survey asks therapists whether they would cover a colleague's client or see an unknown one-off client. The indirect signals cut both ways. Therapists want clients and peer networks. But a one-off brings a full intake, a risk screen and a note for one fee, and some therapist commentary explicitly endorses "declining last-minute walk-ins" ([Anti-Work Therapist](https://antiworktherapist.substack.com/p/some-questions-for-you-to-consider)). Covering a trusted colleague's client, with a handoff in hand, will almost certainly be more acceptable than an anonymous on-demand stranger. That is why the recommendation leads with coverage circles.

**Third, the cold start compounds state by state.** A two-sided marketplace needs density, and licensure splits the network into one small market per state. Mental Health Match had 2,000 listed therapists and still only 119 paying customers. The answer is value for a single therapist before any network exists, through coverage-circle tooling, automated cancellation policies and the context packet, plus density in one state before expanding to the next.

**Fourth, a single adverse event could end the company.** A bridge session puts a clinician with no history in front of a client during a disruption, which is exactly when risk rises. The screening, exclusion and follow-up rules above are the mitigation. Malpractice coverage for single telehealth sessions under registrations or compact privileges also needs confirming; the research retrieved no insurer policy language on it.

**Fifth, compliance drifts.** Licensure rules change monthly. If the engine routes a client to the wrong clinician, the therapist has practiced without a license. Privacy and AI law layer on federal HIPAA and FTC duties, state health-data laws and new consent rules. The compliance cost is large relative to small per-session revenue, so a single-state launch is the only manageable start.

**Sixth, incumbents could close the gap first.** Spring Health already calls continuity the "hardest barrier," and Grow and SimplePractice already ship client summaries. SimplePractice holds data from more than 245,000 practitioners ([Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/new-report-highlights-independent-mental-health-practice-access-to-care-and-evolving-role-of-ai)) and already offers an out-of-office tool, so it could add coverage circles as a feature. A startup's defense is to be independent of any single EHR and aligned with therapists, and to treat partnership or acquisition by one of these companies as a realistic good outcome.

**Seventh, a shutdown would strand clients.** Mindstrong ended patient care about two months after announcing its closure ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/mental-health-platform-mindstrong-close-hq-lay-128-employees)). A product whose purpose is continuity cannot be the cause of a break in care, so data export and a wind-down plan belong in version one.

## A class project and a launch need different scopes

The recommendation holds either way, but scope, rigor and success criteria change sharply depending on whether this is a final project or the start of a company.

| Dimension | Class final project | Planned launch |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Show that the reframing rests on evidence and that the design is credible | Reach a group of paying therapists in one state, then sell bridge coverage |
| What to build | A clickable prototype of the three flows: coverage-circle setup, a bridge booking with a jurisdiction check, and a context packet with a review screen for the return summary | A production system whose first release delivers single-therapist value (coverage circles, cancellation automation, context packet) before opening bridge bookings |
| Jurisdiction engine | A rules table for about five contrasting states, such as California (30-day exception for existing clients), New York and Texas (no pathway), Florida (registration) and an Arizona/Ohio compact pair | A maintained rules engine covering every license type in the launch state and its main travel feeder states, with legal review of every rule |
| Data | Synthetic personas only; no real clients, sessions or health information | A HIPAA-grade video vendor and hosting under business-associate agreements, a security program, breach procedures that meet both HIPAA and FTC rules, and no ad tracking |
| Legal work | A written compliance plan covering the points in this report | Health-care counsel before the first real session on business-associate status, the FTC breach rule, state health-data laws, licensure rules, AI consent, the fee structure, contractor classification and professional-entity rules; plus malpractice carrier confirmation |
| Validation | 8–12 therapist interviews, 5–8 client interviews, and optionally a usability test of a swipe deck against a needs-based intake | The same interviews, then a paid pilot with 30–50 therapists, logs of how often gaps occur, and at least one letter of intent from an institution such as a university |
| Original concept | Can keep the travel persona and the "Tinder" shorthand in the pitch, as long as the evidence against both is presented honestly | Drops both from external branding; travel survives only as routing logic |
| Success test | Graders can follow the path from evidence to design decisions | Therapists pay and renew; covered clients return to their regular therapist; no safety incidents |

For a class project, the strongest deliverable is not polish but the argument. The app began as a matching problem and turned into a continuity and legal-routing problem, and each design rule traces to a source. The interviews matter more than the prototype, because they generate evidence no published source has.

For a launch, the sequence matters more than features. The therapist side comes first: recruit enough therapists in one state that coverage circles work without any client demand, then open bridge sessions to those therapists' own clients and waitlists. Institutional pilots follow, and a second state only after that. The funding trend favors payer-funded recurring-care models, so a launch should plan for modest capital and revenue that institutions pay, not for a burst of paid consumer growth.

Two signals would justify a pivot. The first is therapists refusing to pay a membership. The second is fewer gaps per therapist each year than the coverage pitch assumes. Either one points toward selling coverage tooling to group practices or to an EHR, or toward a university break-coverage product, rather than toward a consumer marketplace.

## Questions to put to therapists before writing code

The research left these questions open, and conversations with therapists can answer them. Each one maps to an assumption that could break the model.

| Question to ask therapists | Assumption it tests | How to get a usable answer |
|---|---|---|
| How did you arrange coverage for your last two absences, how many absences do you take a year, and what went wrong? | Coverage circles solve a frequent, felt problem | Ask about specific recent events, not hypotheticals; count absences and failures |
| Would you see a trusted colleague's client for one covering session? What would you need first: a handoff note, risk history, or the right to exclude certain clients? | Supply exists for sanctioned coverage | Show a mock context packet and handoff note and ask what is missing |
| Would you take a one-off session with someone you don't know, referred by a platform? At what minimum take-home rate, and with what pre-screening? | Bridge sessions have supply beyond the circles | Ask for a dollar figure and a list of deal-breakers |
| How often in the past year did a client travel or move somewhere you couldn't legally see them, and what did you actually do? | Travel demand is meaningful and not already handled informally | Ask for counts; note whether therapists continue informally despite the rules |
| How many late cancellations do you get a week, with how much notice, and would you post those slots or keep the time for notes? | Slot-filling is a real source of supply | Have 3–5 therapists keep a two-week log |
| What must a return summary contain to be useful, how long would writing one take, and would you accept one routed through the client? | The handoff is workable without adding documentation burden | Walk through a mock summary and time the edit |
| Would you read journal excerpts a client chose to share before a session? What liability concern would stop you? | Journal sharing on a push model is acceptable to clinicians | Probe for guidance from their malpractice carrier or board |
| Would you pay a flat monthly fee or a per-session percentage, and at what price would you cancel? | Therapist membership is the primary revenue | Ask for a "too expensive" price and a "suspiciously cheap" price, then offer a paid pilot slot |
| Does your malpractice policy cover a single telehealth session with a client located in another state under a registration or compact privilege? | Bridge sessions can be insured | Ask therapists to check with their carrier; the research found no policy language |
| Would you name covering colleagues if your client might choose to switch to them? | Fear of losing clients won't block referrals | Test a "return to your regular therapist" default and a non-solicitation norm |

Clients should be asked parallel questions. How often have you had a gap in care, and what did you do? Would you pay your covering therapist's rate for one session with a clinician your own therapist recommended, and would you pay it for a stranger? Would you write a context packet and share it? One institutional conversation, ideally with a university counseling center that loses students to state lines every break, tests the third revenue line early.

## Conclusion

The original concept treated therapy access as a discovery problem that a better interface could fix. The evidence reframes it as a **continuity and legal-routing problem**. Clients drop out of care at predictable breaks: their therapist goes on leave, they cross a state line, a waitlist runs for months. No existing system carries their context across those breaks or knows which clinician may lawfully see them that week. The most valuable assets in this space are therefore not a matching algorithm or an AI summary, which incumbents already have. They are a maintained jurisdiction engine and a record the client owns. The most important customer is not the traveler but the solo therapist, who already carries an ethical duty to plan for interruptions and has no tool for it.

The window is narrow. Spring Health, Grow and SimplePractice are each moving toward "continuity" from inside their own walls. A startup's advantage lasts only as long as it stays independent of any one EHR and aligned with therapists. Whether this becomes a company, a feature someone else acquires, or a strong final project depends on numbers nobody has published: how often care is interrupted, and what therapists will pay to stop it. Those numbers come from interviews, and the same interviews serve the class project and the launch.
