## 1. EHR client portals (SimplePractice, TherapyNotes, TheraNest, Jane, Valant, Owl, TherapyAppointment): what therapists can share, limits, complaints

### Takeaway
Therapy EHR portals are mainly administrative tools for intake forms, consents, questionnaires, scheduling, billing and (sometimes) secure messaging. Each one is a per-practice silo that the clinician owns. Client-facing *session summaries* are only now arriving: Upheal's portal in March 2026 and SimplePractice "Client Summaries" in June 2026. Nothing found lets a client carry content from one practice's portal to another.

### Cited Findings
- **SimplePractice portal:** clinicians send intake forms and documents from the client profile, and the client gets an email with a sign-on link to the portal ([SimplePractice support](https://support.simplepractice.com/hc/en-us/articles/360020622052-Getting-started-with-SimplePractice)). A third-party profile says clients can complete intake forms, consents and questionnaires, and view or download invoices, statements and superbills ([IntuitionLabs SimplePractice Portal profile](https://intuitionlabs.ai/software/pdfs/simplepractice-portal.pdf)).
- **SimplePractice Secure Messaging** is available only on the Essential and Plus plans. It must be enabled at both the practice level and the individual-client level. Clients get an email containing a portal login link when a new message arrives, so they don't see message content in the email ([SimplePractice help center](https://support.simplepractice.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003629583)). A competitor (BloomText, which sells texting) calls the forced portal login a friction point ([BloomText](https://www.bloomtext.com/does-simplepractice-have-patient-texting/)). That is a vendor claim.
- **SimplePractice Client Summaries (June 2026):** a press release describes "Client Summaries" as a more client-friendly recap that clinicians can send "in just a few clicks", separate from the structured progress notes Note Taker produces ([Business Wire, June 17 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260617564693/en/); help article: [Using Client Summaries](https://support.simplepractice.com/hc/en-us/articles/45207035372045-Using-Client-Summaries)). Both pages returned 403 to direct fetch, so detailed mechanics such as review steps, pricing and portal versus email delivery were not verified.
- **Upheal Client Portal (launched Mar 31 2026):** clients can self-book, reschedule or cancel (if enabled), join sessions, complete forms, view past sessions, and view any session summaries the therapist has shared. Secure messaging, native iOS/Android apps and client billing are listed as "coming later". No journaling, homework or measures are mentioned ([Upheal changelog](https://feedback.upheal.io/changelog/your-clients-now-have-their-own-upheal-portal-fire)).
- **TherapyNotes:** the portal lets clinicians share documents and connect with clients. Secure messaging is listed as a core feature, and G2 reviewers rate its usability highly. These come from affiliate-style comparisons ([Mentalyc TheraNest vs TherapyNotes](https://www.mentalyc.com/blog/?p=372); [G2 comparison](https://www.g2.com/compare/theranest-by-ensora-health-vs-therapynotes); [TrustRadius](https://trustradius.com/compare-products/therapy-brands-theranest-vs-therapynotes)).
- **TheraNest (Ensora):** the portal offers self-scheduling, intake forms, statements and payments. Reviewers cite limited customization of documentation, assessments and forms, and one says the "client portal as well as billing abilities are not as robust as other programs" ([CarePaths TheraNest review](https://carepaths.com/theranest-review/)).
- **Jane:** the "Jane for Clients" app handles messaging, resources, receipts, forms and payments, and telehealth is built in ([Jane mental health page](https://jane.app/mentalhealth)). A review praises customizable intake forms and a shared template library ([Choosing Therapy Jane review](https://www.choosingtherapy.com/jane-app-review/)). Jane does not send traditional SMS and uses an in-app messaging hub instead (same review).
- **TherapyAppointment:** the only detail found was a reviewer praising client messaging inside the portal ([Capterra comparison](https://www.capterra.com/compare/110508-178984/TherapyAppointment-vs-Jane-App)).
- **Owl Insights (measurement-based care):** helps providers "screen, track, analyze and guide treatment" with patient-reported outcome measures. Its vendor-stated outcomes (90% patient engagement, 50% shorter treatment, 48% fewer no-shows) are marketing claims that have not been independently verified ([Kando profile](https://kando.tech/company/owl-insights)). It is unclear whether "Owl Practice" (the Canadian practice-management EHR) is the same company.
- **Valant:** a behavioral-health EHR whose customers describe using it for measurement-based care ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/valant-medical-solutions/customers)). No portal-specific detail was found.

### Inferences
- Portal design across these EHRs assumes one client and one practice. A client who sees a one-off or covering therapist ends up with another portal login and no shared context. That fragmentation is the gap a matching app could address, not the portal functions themselves, which are commoditized.
- Client-facing session summaries are becoming a standard EHR feature in 2026 (Upheal, SimplePractice). A matching app should not build its pitch on "therapists can share summaries with clients", because incumbents already offer it. The differentiator would be *cross-provider* summaries the client keeps.
- Gating secure messaging by plan tier and by per-client switch suggests vendors and therapists treat between-session messaging as something to ration, which fits the liability and boundary concerns in Section 5.

### Gaps
- No Reddit (r/therapists, r/TalkTherapy) threads on portal complaints were retrieved. Complaint evidence here comes from review sites and competitor marketing only.
- Valant, Owl Practice and TherapyAppointment portal feature sets were not verified from their own documentation.
- Whether SimplePractice Client Summaries go through clinician review, whether they are AI-generated by default, and how they reach the client (portal or email) were not confirmed because the pages were inaccessible.

## 2. Sharing notes and summaries with clients (OpenNotes in mental health and psychotherapy)

### Takeaway
Across surveys, qualitative studies and an expert Delphi panel, the evidence shows patients mostly benefit from reading mental-health notes: more trust, understanding, sense of control and medication adherence. A small minority report more worry, and harms cluster around inaccurate, stigmatizing or "surprise" content. Clinicians are more worried than patients, and documentation measurably changes (simpler, sometimes omitting sensitive judgment) once patients can read it. Psychotherapy notes in the HIPAA sense remain legally exempt from mandatory sharing.

### Cited Findings
- **Blease, Walker, Torous and O'Neill, Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2020:** they argue that sharing psychotherapy notes can support informed consent, recall and patient autonomy. They note that psychotherapy notes are exempt from the US federal rules mandating note sharing. They cite a study of 85 patients with psychotherapy-note access in which more than half said reading notes was very or extremely important for feeling in control ([Frontiers in Psychiatry 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.527872/epub)). The 85-patient study itself was not independently located.
- **Blease (Psychology Today, Apr 2020)** summarizes a study of patients with access to their therapists' documentation in which the "overwhelming majority" thought access was a good idea and wanted it to continue ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/digital-mental-health/202004/should-psychotherapists-open-their-notes-patients)).
- **International Delphi panel (PLOS ONE)** reached consensus on "multiple benefits and few harms" from patients with mental illness reading their notes. Experts agreed that *blocking* notes could cause greater harm, including feelings of stigmatization. They also predicted some patients would demand changes, and that clinicians might document negative aspects less accurately or in less detail ([MDedge/The Hospitalist coverage](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/open-notes-big-benefits-few-harms-psychiatry-experts-say)).
- **O'Neill, Blease and Delbanco (policy viewpoint, 2021)** say they are not aware of any reports of harm to, or legal action from, patients accessing their mental-health notes ([Blease PDF](https://charlotteblease.com/_files/ugd/510dea_6d25af180e714a788b487c4a6022137d.pdf)). The same source gives a figure for how many of 300+ open-notes health systems also shared mental-health notes, but the search snippet garbled it as ".25%", so it is not reported here.
- **Blease et al., JAMA Network Open, Mar 2021** (survey reanalysis, patients with serious mental illness who read at least one note): SMI patients read notes at a lower rate than patients without a mental-health diagnosis (47% vs 62%). Of SMI patients, 20% said reading notes made them more likely to take medications, versus 14% without a diagnosis, and 67% said notes helped them understand why medications were prescribed. Patients with other mental-health diagnoses were slightly more likely to feel more worried (5% vs 3%). The data are self-reported, and response bias is possible ([JAMA Netw Open 2021;4(3):e212823](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2777857)).
- **VA (Cromer, Denneson, … Dobscha, Psychiatric Services, 2017)** ran qualitative interviews with 28 veterans with depression, PTSD, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia. Trust rose when notes matched the session, showed the clinician had listened, and recorded the patient's strengths. Trust fell with mismatches, missing or wrong details, and copied-forward outdated text. The authors recommend that clinicians talk proactively about note content and document strengths ([VA Research Currents](https://www.research.va.gov/currents/0217-8.cfm); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170301105622.htm)). Related papers: Denneson et al. 2018 (*Psychiatric Services*) on positive and negative responses, and Dobscha et al. 2018 (*JAMIA Open*) on predictors of use (same sources).
- **Schwarz et al., JMIR Mental Health, Dec 2021 (scoping review):** service users mostly reported benefits, including more trust, health literacy and empowerment. Negative experiences were tied to inaccurate notes, disrespectful language, or discovering diagnoses that had not been discussed. For clinicians, concerns outweighed perceived benefits: more documentation burden and possible harm from reading. The authors call the evidence base limited ([JMIR Ment Health 2021;8(12):e34170](https://mental.jmir.org/2021/12/e34170)).
- **JMIR 2025 scoping review on documentation changes:** after patients get online record access, notes become less complex, more readable and different in emotional tone. The review warns that access "may also compromise the integrity of documentation by omitting sensitive findings and expert judgment". Compensatory practices such as parallel documentation and restricted access point to systemic problems ([JMIR 2025;e64762](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e64762)). **This is the strongest disconfirming evidence: open notes can degrade what gets written down.**
- **Legal frame:** psychotherapy notes as HIPAA defines them (kept separately from the rest of the record) are excluded from "electronic health information" under the Cures Act information-blocking rules. Patients can still access most other mental-health records unless information is withheld to prevent harm ([CHCANYS Cures Act guide](https://www.chcanys.org/sites/default/files/2025-04/Cures%20Act%20Basics%20for%20Providers%20%28Long%29.docx); [Houston Methodist FAQ](https://uatit.houstonmethodist.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Cures-Act-Open-Notes-Sharing-FAQs_Final_111621.pdf)). Some psychiatrists have over-redacted released records beyond what the Cures Act requires ([Massachusetts Psychiatric Society](https://www.psychiatry-mps.org/assets/Resources/2020/10-2020/Dr.%20Reyering%20Nov-Dec%202020.pdf)). A behavioral-health EHR vendor advises keeping psychotherapy notes physically separate ([Qualifacts](https://www.qualifacts.com/resources/mental-health-providers-information-blocking/)).
- **After-visit summaries in behavioral health (product level):** Upheal generates a client-facing "post-session summary" only when a transcript exists. Summaries are sharable by PDF or email (Aug 2024) and through the client portal (since Mar 2026), and are included in Premium/Unlimited plans as of 2024. Upheal's separate "Treatment Summary" is explicitly *not* shown to clients ([Upheal support](https://support.upheal.io/en/articles/9813366-post-session-summaries-for-clients); [Upheal Aug 2024 update](https://upheal.io/recent-updates/summaries-for-your-clients-are-here); [Upheal Treatment Summaries](https://support.upheal.io/en/articles/11954872-about-treatment-summaries)). Rula publishes a therapist guide to session summaries ([Rula](https://therapistsupport.rula.com/hc/en-us/articles/54847643171611-Session-summary-a-guide-for-therapists)), and NovoPsych's AI scribe has a "Client Session Summary and Homework Activities" template ([NovoPsych](https://novopsych.com/novonote-ai-scribe-template/client-document/note-template-client-session-summary-homework/)). Neither page was opened, so only the titles are confirmed.

### Inferences
- A *client-facing summary written for the client* is safer and more useful than exposing the clinical note. It avoids the documentation-distortion and "surprise diagnosis" harms while keeping the trust and recall benefits. The VA findings suggest such summaries should be accurate to the session, mention strengths, and avoid stale copied text.
- For a one-off session, the content most valuable to the regular therapist (clinical impressions, risk observations) is also the content most likely to sting if the client reads it unedited. Handoff design probably needs two layers: what the client sees, and what goes clinician-to-clinician. The client should know both exist.
- The direct evidence base for psychotherapy specifically, as opposed to psychiatry or general mental-health notes, is thin and mostly survey or qualitative.

### Gaps
- No RCT on sharing psychotherapy notes or summaries with clients was found.
- The current (2025–2026) share of US health systems sharing mental-health notes could not be verified.
- No empirical study was found on client-facing *AI-generated* session summaries in therapy, covering accuracy, client reactions or harms.
- Evidence on "collaborative documentation" (writing notes with the client in session) could not be retrieved before the search budget ran out.

## 3. AI scribes and summaries (Upheal, Mentalyc, Blueprint, Freed, Heidi, SimplePractice Note Taker)

### Takeaway
AI scribes are now mainstream in therapy practice. Roughly 30–60% of therapists report some AI use, though estimates vary widely and measure different things. Privacy concern is rising, not falling. A few scribes now generate client-facing summaries (Upheal; SimplePractice Client Summaries; Blueprint's between-session summaries are clinician-facing). The main risks are consent to recording, vendor data retention or training, and accuracy that requires clinician editing. Ambient-scribe consent litigation began in late 2025.

### Cited Findings
- **APA 2025 Practitioner Pulse (reported Mar 2026):** uptake is increasing but caution persists, with privacy concerns alongside growing comfort using AI for practice management ([APA Monitor, Mar 2026](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2026/03/ai-reshaping-therapy)). Coverage of the same survey (1,700+ psychologists, Sep 2025) puts concern about data-privacy violations at 67%, up from 59% the year before, and reports about half experimenting with AI ([heise](https://heise.de/-11113260)).
- **Adoption estimates conflict.** A vendor report says 62% of therapists used AI tools in 2026 ([Berries 2026 report, vendor](https://heyberries.com/the-2026-state-of-ai-in-mental-health-practice-report)). Other coverage cites 29% using AI monthly to record or transcribe sessions and quotes the APA's Vaile Wright saying that because regulation is thin, "it is incumbent on the provider to do the legwork". The same coverage reports nearly 40 companies offering therapy transcription and documentation ([inkl, "patients say they never signed up for a third listener"](https://www.inkl.com/news/ai-is-taking-over-therapy-notes-but-patients-say-they-never-signed-up-for-a-third-listener)).
- **Public trust is low:** a YouGov poll reported only 11% of Americans open to AI for mental-health care and 8% trusting it. A KFF survey found 77% worried about how AI systems store and use their health information. Both are as reported in secondary coverage ([claimer summary page](https://claimer-org.github.io/claimer/); [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/49480-can-an-ai-chatbot-be-your-therapist?stream=top)). Treat the exact figures cautiously.
- **SimplePractice Note Taker** records telehealth sessions (or takes uploaded audio or dictated summaries) and drafts SOAP/DAP/BIRP notes inside the EHR. Notes require clinician review and attestation, and explicit informed client consent is required. Third-party pricing sources conflict, ranging from about $17.50 to $35 per clinician per month, including the "Care Aide" bundle ([Commure review](https://commure.com/blog-scribe/simplepractice-ai-notes); [Dupple review](https://dupple.com/reviews/simplepractice)). Client Summaries are the client-facing companion feature (Section 1).
- **Upheal:** generates progress notes and client-facing post-session summaries (transcript required). Therapists can pause the AI for sensitive topics and create anonymous clients. Upheal claims explicit opt-in before any data is used for training and accuses Mentalyc's policy of implicitly allowing training; this is a competitor claim ([Upheal vs Mentalyc, vendor](https://www.upheal.io/blog/upheal-vs-mentalyc)).
- **Mentalyc:** offers recording, post-session dictation or file upload. It claims most notes export without substantive edits but says clinical specifics should be clinician-verified ([Mentalyc AI notes](https://www.mentalyc.com/ai-therapy-notes)). Mentalyc's blog says Upheal's accuracy and editing time are a bottleneck; this is a competitor claim ([Mentalyc Upheal review](https://www.mentalyc.com/blog/upheal-reviews)).
- **Blueprint:** transcribes sessions into notes and treatment plans, collects between-session data (assessments, worksheets such as CBT thought records and DBT diary cards, sleep, exercise), and generates a *clinician-facing* summary for the start of the next session ([AdvancedMD marketplace listing](https://www.advancedmd.com/integrations/marketplace/blueprint)). It offers 250+ validated assessments ([Fast.io review](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-for-therapists-2026/)). A competitor blog reports a free EHR with per-session AI pricing ($0.99–$1.49) and user complaints about post-session editing to fix attribution errors ([Mentalyc Blueprint reviews, competitor](https://www.mentalyc.com/blog/blueprint-reviews)). No client-facing session summary from Blueprint was confirmed.
- **Talkspace** (Jan 2025) launched clinician-facing "AI-Powered Insights" to help providers prep for sessions and guide care between sessions ([Talkspace release via Manila Times/GlobeNewswire](https://manilatimes.net/2025/01/15/tmt-newswire/globenewswire/talkspace-launches-ai-powered-insights-to-advance-provider-efficiency-and-enhance-therapeutic-care/2039462)).
- **Consent litigation:** a proposed class action (filed late Nov or Dec 2025; sources differ) alleges Sharp HealthCare used Abridge's ambient scribe to record visits without consent, falsely documented consent, and that the vendor retained audio for 30 days. The plaintiff discovered the recording by reading his own visit note. Plaintiffs estimate about 100,000 recorded encounters. The allegations are unproven ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/healthcare-information-technology/ai/patient-sues-sharp-healthcare-over-ambient-ai-use/); [HealthExec](https://healthexec.com/topics/artificial-intelligence/lawsuit-claims-clinic-used-ai-record-patient-conversations-without-consent)). This is general medicine, not therapy, but it is directly relevant precedent.
- Professional codes (NASW, AAMFT) require informed or written consent before audio recording, and recording consent is a legal requirement in most states, as summarized by a vendor ([Upheal guide](https://upheal.io/documentation/how-to-choose-an-ai-note-taking-tool-for-therapy-a-step-by-step-guide)).

### Inferences
- Scribe-generated client summaries are a low-cost way for a one-off therapist to produce a client recap and a clinician handoff from the same session. They also inherit the scribe's consent burden, and some clients will refuse recording. A matching app would need a no-recording path (therapist-typed or dictated summary) as a first-class option.
- Since most therapists already run an EHR with an AI scribe, a matching app that requires therapists to re-document in a new system will meet resistance. Accepting an export or paste from the therapist's existing scribe is likely lower friction.
- Rising privacy concern (67%) and low public trust (about 8–11%) suggest "AI-written summary" should not be the headline feature for clients. Human-reviewed summaries are the safer framing.

### Gaps
- Freed and Heidi features and therapy adoption were not researched (search budget exhausted).
- No independent (non-vendor) accuracy or hallucination study of therapy-specific scribes was found.
- No r/therapists sentiment data was retrieved.

## 4. Homework and between-session platforms; measurement-based care (MBC)

### Takeaway
The evidence that *doing* homework predicts better CBT outcomes is solid, as is the evidence that routine outcome monitoring modestly improves results, especially for clients who aren't progressing. Platforms (Blueprint, Owl, NOCD's app plus messaging) operationalize both. MBC is chronically under-used, and digital homework depends on client completion.

### Cited Findings
- **Kazantzis et al. 2016 (Behavior Therapy 47:755–772):** a meta-analysis of homework compliance and outcome across about 17 CBT studies (~2,300 clients). Quantity of compliance was associated with outcome at post-treatment (g = 0.79, k = 7) and follow-up (g = 0.51, k = 7). Quality estimates were larger but rest on only 3 studies with wide confidence intervals ([Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/quantity-and-quality-of-homework-compliance-a-meta-analysis-of-re/)). This is correlational: compliance predicts outcome but does not prove that assigning homework through an app causes it.
- **Routine outcome monitoring / feedback (Barkham, De Jong, Delgadillo, Lutz 2023 review of 11 meta-analyses):** statistically significant but modest effects (g ≈ 0.15, about an 8-point success-rate advantage). Effects are larger for not-on-track patients (d ≈ 0.29) and larger still when clinical support tools are added (g ≈ 0.36–0.53). Implementation is a "major challenge", feedback "continues to be under-utilized in routine care", and findings across meta-analyses have diverged ([White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/197689/13/Routine%20Outcome%20Monitoring%20ROM%20and%20Feedback%20Research%20Review%20and%20Recommendations.pdf)).
- **NOCD (OCD, ERP plus app plus between-session therapist messaging):** a retrospective JMIR 2022 study of 3,552 adults found 38.9% mean symptom reduction and a 52% response rate with about 10.6 therapist hours, maintained at 3–12 months. It was company-affiliated and uncontrolled ([JMIR 2022;24(5):e36431](https://jmir.org/2022/5/e36431)). A preprint of 2,069 patients reported about 45% reduction and 70% response ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.01.18.20173633.full.pdf)).
- **Blueprint** bundles digital homework (thought records, diary cards), PROMs (PHQ-9, GAD-7) and dashboards. Reviewers caution that outcome visibility is "only as good as client completion" ([AdvancedMD listing](https://www.advancedmd.com/integrations/marketplace/blueprint); [Fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-for-therapists-2026/)).
- **Owl Insights** sells MBC with a large library of measures. Its outcome claims are vendor marketing ([Kando](https://kando.tech/company/owl-insights)).
- **Headspace's "Ebb"** AI companion is positioned for "reflection and everyday support between therapy sessions" for Headspace therapy members (June 2025) ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2025/06/18/headspace-launches-insurance-covered-therapy-direct-to-consumers); [Headspace help](https://help.headspace.com/hc/en-us/articles/37974526207643-What-are-the-benefits-of-online-therapy)).

### Inferences
- For a one-off session, homework and MBC tools mostly matter as *continuity artifacts*. A PHQ-9/GAD-7 trend and a "what we worked on, what to practice" note are compact, standardized and useful to the regular therapist. They are good candidates for passport and handoff content.
- MBC's biggest benefit is for clients not on track. A cross-provider score history could flag that pattern, but only if the client's regular therapist is also measuring, which is often not the case.

### Gaps
- Quenza and Therapist Aid were not researched (feature sets, usage, pricing).
- No current figure was retrieved for the share of US therapists routinely using standardized measures (the search was blocked by the budget limit).
- No independent RCTs of between-session homework *apps*, as opposed to homework compliance generally, were located.

## 5. Journaling: evidence, products, therapist-connected journals, monitoring risks

### Takeaway
Expressive writing has a real but small average effect, roughly d ≈ 0.15 across broad samples. For depression specifically, a 2018 meta-analysis found no benefit from brief self-directed expressive writing. Effects look larger in narrower clinical groups such as diagnosed PTSD. Consumer journaling and mood apps abound, but mental-health apps in general retain only about 3–4% of users at 15–30 days. A handful of small products let clients share journal entries with therapists, using very different models: full entries, summaries only, or metadata only. Giving therapists access to journals creates implied-monitoring liability.

### Cited Findings
- **Frattaroli 2006 meta-analysis:** small but significant average effect, r = .075, often restated as d ≈ 0.15 ([Frattaroli PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/2006-frattaroli.pdf); [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2023 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1204053/pdf)). Earlier fixed-effects meta-analyses (Smyth 1998, d = 0.47) overstated effects (same sources).
- **Adolescents:** Travagin et al. 2015 found g = 0.127 ([OJP abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/library/publications/how-effective-are-expressive-writing-interventions-adolescents-meta-analytic)).
- **PTSD:** Pavlacic et al. 2019 found small overall effects on posttraumatic stress, but medium-to-large effects in studies requiring a PTSD diagnosis (as summarized in search results drawing on [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1204053/pdf)).
- **Depression (disconfirming):** Reinhold, Bürkner and Holling 2018 (Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice), across 39 studies, "did not support the effectiveness of brief, self-directed expressive writing" for depressive symptoms. Longer spacing between sessions was beneficial ([Reinhold et al. PDF](https://paul-buerkner.github.io/publications/pdf/2018__Reinhold_et_al__Clinical_Psychology_Science_and_Practice.pdf)).
- **2023 meta-analysis (31 trials, healthy or subclinical samples):** small effect on depression, anxiety and stress (g = −0.12, 95% CI −0.21 to −0.04). More research is needed in clinical samples ([Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1192595/full)).
- **App retention (disconfirming):** Baumel et al. 2019 (JMIR, 93 mental-health apps, panel data) found median 15-day retention of 3.9% and 30-day retention of 3.3% despite a median of 100,000 installs. A secondary source reports a median daily open rate of 4.0%, higher for trackers (6.3%) and peer support (17.0%) ([JMIR 2019;21(9):e14567](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/E14567/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785720)).
- **Rosebud (AI journal):** raised a $6M seed in June 2025, reportedly led by Bessemer, with plans to explore school, business and clinic partnerships ([TechCrunch, Jun 4 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/04/rosebud-lands-6m-to-scale-its-interactive-ai-journaling-app)). A May 2026 Rosebud blog post (vendor) advertises "shareable weekly reports" at $12.99/mo so that insights "don't stay locked in an app" ([Rosebud blog](https://www.rosebud.app/blog/mental-wellness-apps-with-journaling-for-people-in-therapy)). Long-term memory is paywalled ([Macaron review 2026](https://macaron.im/blog/rosebud-ai-journaling-app-review-2026)). Rosebud says entries are not shared with third parties or used for training (company claim, same sources). No independent confirmation of a therapist-specific sharing feature was found.
- **How We Feel:** free, run by a nonprofit (How We Feel Project), with product led by Pinterest co-founder Ben Silbermann and science from Marc Brackett's Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence. Features include emotion check-ins, pattern reports, regulation strategies, a "friends" sharing feature, HealthKit sleep and exercise, and PDF export of check-ins ([Yale Medicine](https://medicine.yale.edu/news-article/the-how-we-feel-app-helping-emotions-work-for-us-not-against-us/); [Marc Brackett](https://marcbrackett.com/how-we-feel-app-3/); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1562706384)).
- **Day One:** privacy-leading, with end-to-end encryption and no server-side AI analysis. Premium costs about $34.99/yr on iOS, per low-authority comparison blogs ([Journal Gems](https://journal-gems-blog.pages.dev/blog/best-journaling-apps/)). **Daylio:** tap-based mood and activity tracking, with entries under 30 seconds ([Reflection comparison, self-interested](https://www.reflection.app/best-journaling-apps-compared/daylio-vs-stoic)). **Stoic:** structured, philosophy-framed journaling with weak export options ([MyLifeNote, competitor](https://blog.mylifenote.ai/stoic-app-alternative/)).
- **Therapist-connected journaling exists only in niche products, with divergent designs:**
  - **SafeTalk:** the client chooses individual entries to reveal and can withdraw them. The therapist sees "New" and "Read" queues ([SafeTalk](https://safetalk.space/blog/help-centre/how-safetalk-journaling-works-a-therapists-perspective/)).
  - **YouWell Patient Diary:** private by default, with per-entry opt-in visibility ([YouWell](https://support.youwell.no/en/support/solutions/articles/153000244720)).
  - **Empaithy:** shares a weekly report rather than raw entries, with the user controlling duration ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6477714987)).
  - **Theryo:** shares AI summaries with the therapy team ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6736987642)).
  - **Therachat:** deliberately shares only the *date and time* of entries, "to respect the boundaries of the therapy relationship" ([Therachat help](https://therachat.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/151000119081-journal-activity-)).
  - ReachLink and Calmerry (text-therapy platforms) have journal features per their help and blog titles ([ReachLink](https://help.reachlink.com/mobile-app/journals/echoes); [Calmerry](https://calmerry.com/blog/calmerry-news/guided-journaling-release)). Details were not verified.
- **Monitoring and liability risk:** an HPSO risk-management case describes a counselor who received frequent after-hours texts from a client who later died by suicide. The mother demanded a $500,000 settlement, and the licensing board reviewed the text and DM history ([HPSO case study](https://www.hpso.com/Resources/Legal-and-Ethical-Issues/Counselor-Case-Study-Inappropriate-dual-relationship-Failure-to-assign)). A research protocol for therapist-monitored texting used auto-replies outside office hours and trigger-word routing to crisis contacts ([ClinicalTrials.gov protocol NCT04011540](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/40/NCT04011540/Prot_SAP_000.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Journaling's evidence supports it as an *adjunct* (reflection, recall and session prep), not as a treatment. Pitching "journal sharing" as a clinical intervention would overclaim.
- Therachat's metadata-only design and SafeTalk's opt-in-per-entry design are both responses to the same fear: a therapist who *can* read a journal may be expected to read it, including suicidal content written at 2 a.m. For a one-off or covering therapist, who has no ongoing relationship, real-time journal access is especially hazardous. Client-curated, point-in-time excerpts or summaries shared *for a specific session*, with an explicit "not monitored" notice and crisis routing, would limit that exposure.
- Low retention (about 3–4% at 30 days) argues against building the product around daily journaling engagement. Journaling that is event-driven (before or after a session, while traveling) fits a one-off-session use case better.

### Gaps
- Reflectly and Jour were not researched, and no figures were found on usage of journal-to-therapist features or outcomes from them.
- No published evaluation of a therapist-connected journaling product was found. Whether any such feature was tried and failed or was withdrawn is unknown.
- No authoritative legal source was found on whether a therapist with journal access has a duty to monitor. It likely varies by state and by the informed-consent language used.

## 6. AI chatbots and companions as gap-fillers (Woebot, Wysa, Ash, Therabot, ChatGPT; harms and lawsuits)

### Takeaway
General-purpose chatbots are already a mass-scale emotional-support channel. Roughly a quarter to half of relevant US samples report using LLMs for mental-health support. Purpose-built therapy bots, meanwhile, struggle commercially and with regulators: Woebot shut its consumer app in 2025, and Slingshot's Ash left the UK in 2026. One rigorous RCT (Therabot) shows promise. Documented harms include teen suicides that led to lawsuits and settlements, plus a wave of state laws (IL, NV, CO; CA companion-chatbot rules). For a human one-off-session app, chatbots are both a competitor for low-acuity "I need to talk now" demand and a feeder and complement: the human escalation path the bots, regulators and many users want.

### Cited Findings
- **Woebot:** announced in April 2025 that it would retire its consumer app on June 30, 2025. Users could download conversation history until then, and data was anonymized after July 31 ([HLTH, Apr 28 2025](https://hlth.com/insights/news/woebot-health-is-shutting-down-its-app-2025-04-28)). Founder Alison Darcy told STAT the shutdown was "largely attributable to the cost and challenge of fulfilling the FDA's requirements for marketing authorization" and said AI is moving faster than regulators ([STAT, Jul 2 2025](https://www.statnews.com/2025/07/02/woebot-mental-health-chatbot-shuts-down-founder-says-ai-moving-faster-than-regulators/)). Reports that it then pivoted to an enterprise/payer access-code model come from low-authority sites only ([IntuitionLabs](https://intuitionlabs.ai/software/telepsychiatry-digital-mental-health/chatbots-and-ai-therapy-assistants/woebot)).
- **Wysa:** received FDA Breakthrough Device *designation* in May 2022 (chronic pain with depression and anxiety) ([Business Wire 2022](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220512005084/en/Wysa-Receives-FDA-Breakthrough-Device-Designation-for-AI-led-Mental-Health-Conversational-Agent)). Designation is not clearance ([TechCrunch, Jul 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/07/14/wysa-20-million-series-b-funding-expand-therapist-chatbot-wider-mental-health-services)). Wysa markets "Wysa Copilot", a hybrid digital-and-human therapy platform; the launch date was not verified.
- **Slingshot AI's Ash:** launched July 2025 as a free app after raising $93M (a16z, Forerunner, Radical) ([Behavioral Health Business, Jul 22 2025](https://bhbusiness.com/2025/07/22/with-93m-raised-slingshot-slingshot-ai-debuts-ai-powered-therapy-service/)). STAT questioned whether the company's evidence shows Ash does more good than harm ([STAT, Jul 2025](https://statnews.com/2025/07/22/slingshot-new-investors-generative-ai-mental-health-therapy-chatbot-called-ash/)). In January 2026 Slingshot withdrew Ash from the UK over medical-device rules, with the CEO saying there is no clear regulatory pathway for wellbeing products. The company claims more than 150,000 users ([STAT, Jan 21 2026](https://www.statnews.com/2026/01/21/slingshot-therapy-chatbot-ash-uk-regulatory-concerns/)).
- **Therabot (Dartmouth; Heinz, Jacobson et al., NEJM AI, Mar 27 2025):** the first RCT of a generative-AI therapy chatbot, with about 210 adults (106 Therabot, 104 waitlist control) with MDD, GAD or eating-disorder risk. After 4 weeks of unlimited access, average reductions were 51% for depression, 31% for anxiety and 19% for ED-risk body/weight concerns, all better than control. Users averaged about 6 hours of use (~8 sessions' worth) and reported alliance comparable to human therapists. Clinicians monitored conversations, and crisis content triggered 911/hotline prompts. About 75% of the Therabot group were in no other treatment. Authors say "no generative AI agent is ready to operate fully autonomously in mental health" ([Dartmouth](https://tto.dartmouth.edu/news/2025/03/first-therapy-chatbot-trial-yields-mental-health-benefits)). MIT Technology Review cautioned that the results don't validate the broader wave of AI therapy bots ([MIT Tech Review, Mar 28 2025](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/03/28/1114001/)). Dartmouth news indexes a 2026 *Science* piece, "The Therabot will see you now" ([Dartmouth Geisel](https://geiselmed.dartmouth.edu/news/2026/the-therabot-will-see-you-now-science/)); its content was not read.
- **Scale of use:**
  - Rousmaniere et al. (Practice Innovations; dated 2025 or June 2026 depending on source) surveyed US adults with ongoing mental-health conditions who used an LLM. Of these, 48.7% used LLMs for psychological support in the past year, 63.4% reported improved mental health, and 82.3% were satisfied with overall helpfulness. This was a Prolific sample, not representative ([NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2026/journal-article-abstracts/pri0000292/)).
  - Other US studies cited in JMIR Mental Health 2026: Stade et al. found 24% of a general adult sample used LLMs for mental-health support, and Ueda et al. found 35% weekly use ([JMIR Ment Health 2026;e104316](https://mental.jmir.org/2026/1/e104316)).
  - AXA/Ipsos 2026 (18 countries) found about 6 in 10 seek mental-health advice from AI, but 45% are dissatisfied with it ([Euronews, Jun 3 2026](https://www.euronews.com/next/2026/06/03/more-than-60-people-use-ai-for-mental-health-support-but-many-are-unhappy-with-it-survey-f)).
  - A UK poll found 37% of adults have used a chatbot for mental-health or wellbeing support ([Healthcare Management UK](https://www.healthcare-management.uk/ai-chatbots-mental-health-support)).
  - Verasight (Sep 2025, n = 353 US AI-for-mental-health users) found 74% would have someone else to talk to if they lost chatbot access ([Verasight](https://data.verasight.io/ai/most-americans-have-someone-besides-an-ai-chatbot-to-talk-to-about-mental-health)).
  - A separate study links heavier chatbot use for personal support with more depression and anxiety. The relationship is correlational ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/mental-health/ai-chatbots-personal-support-linked-depression-anxiety-study-rcna255036)).
- **OpenAI's own data (Oct 27 2025):** about 0.15% of weekly active users show explicit indicators of suicidal planning or intent (~1M+ people per week). About 0.07% (~560,000) show possible signs of psychosis or mania, and about 0.15% show heightened emotional attachment ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/27/openai-says-over-a-million-people-talk-to-chatgpt-about-suicide-weekly)).
- **Lawsuits and harms:**
  - Character.AI said it would bar under-18s from open-ended chats by Nov 25 2025 ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/legal-ethics/character-ai-to-ban-children-from-talking-to-its-chatbots)).
  - In January 2026 Character.AI and Google agreed to settle suits in FL, CO, NY and TX, including Megan Garcia's suit over 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III and the case of 13-year-old Juliana Peralta. The settlements were subject to court approval ([WTOP/AP, Jan 2026](https://wtop.com/national/2026/01/google-and-chatbot-maker-character-to-settle-lawsuit-alleging-chatbot-pushed-teen-to-suicide/); [Euronews](https://euronews.com/next/2026/01/08/google-and-chatbot-start-up-characterai-to-settle-lawsuits-over-teen-suicides)).
  - OpenAI faces the Raine suit (16-year-old Adam Raine; the family alleges ChatGPT acted as a "suicide coach", and an amended complaint alleges weakened self-harm safeguards) plus other suits, including a 23-year-old Texas graduate student's death. OpenAI denies responsibility (same sources). Seven further suits against OpenAI were reportedly filed in November 2025 by the Social Media Victims Law Center, per a low-authority tracker ([tracker](https://humanoidliability.pages.dev/resources/character-ai-wrongful-death)).
  - The FTC opened an inquiry in September 2025 into seven companies, including Character.AI, Google, Meta, Snap, OpenAI and xAI ([WTOP/AP](https://wtop.com/national/2026/01/google-and-chatbot-maker-character-to-settle-lawsuit-alleging-chatbot-pushed-teen-to-suicide/)).
- **Regulation:**
  - **Illinois** (WOPR Act, signed Aug 2025) bars AI from independent therapeutic decisions, from treatment plans without licensed review, and from detecting emotions or mental states. Fines run up to $10,000 per use ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/healthcare-information-technology/illinois-bans-ai-therapy-without-clinician-oversight/); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/illinois-bans-ai-from-providing-therapy-2000639042)).
  - **Nevada** bans AI acting as a mental-health professional. **Utah** requires disclosure rather than a ban ([CASRAI comparison](https://casrai.org/compare/ai-therapy-laws-illinois-nevada-utah-compared)).
  - **Colorado HB26-1195** (reportedly signed Jun 3 2026, effective about Aug 2026) keeps psychotherapy with licensed clinicians, allows limited AI for operational support, and leaves "direct psychotherapy" undefined pending regulation. This comes mainly from an AI-written newsletter plus a law-firm roundup, so verify ([Mind The Model](https://buttondown.com/mindthemodel/archive/colorados-ai-psychotherapy-law-two-months-in-what/); [Orrick 2026 state chatbot laws](https://www.orrick.com/fr-FR/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends)).
  - **California SB 243** (effective Jan 1 2026) requires companion-chatbot AI disclosure, suicide and self-harm protocols, minor protections and a private right of action ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/34261-law-on-companion-chatbots-sb-243-enters-into-force-with-grace-period)). A successor, "Adam's Law" (SB 1119), was reportedly signed Sep 10 2026, but this is single-sourced and unverified.
  - The WSJ (Aug 2026) reported that state laws generally lack exemptions for clinical-grade products, quoting a Dartmouth psychiatrist ([Dartmouth Geisel](https://geiselmed.dartmouth.edu/news/2026/the-wild-west-of-ai-therapy-laws-the-wall-street-journal-whats-news/)).
- **FDA Digital Health Advisory Committee (Nov 6 2025)** considered generative-AI mental-health devices (a hypothetical prescription LLM chatbot for MDD). It recommended risk-based premarket evidence, human escalation pathways, consent and labeling, and postmarket adverse-event surveillance. FDA noted that more than 1,200 AI devices are authorized but none use generative AI for mental-health conditions ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/11/FDAs-Digital-Health-Advisory-Committee-Considers-Generative-AI-Therapy-Chatbots-for-Depression); [FDA DHAC page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/fda-digital-health-advisory-committee)).
- **Clinical practice is adapting:** a JAMA Psychiatry paper (date unconfirmed, likely 2026) urges clinicians to routinely ask patients about AI-chatbot use for emotional support ([Ground News summary](https://ground.news/article/new-paper-urges-therapists-to-screen-patients-for-ai-chatbot-use)).

### Inferences
- **Competition:** for "I'm traveling and anxious tonight" needs, a free, instant chatbot beats a paid, scheduled human session on price and speed. The AXA/Ipsos dissatisfaction figure (45%) and the lawsuits suggest a meaningful share of users want a human. The one-off app's value proposition is "a licensed human, today", not "support whenever".
- **Complement:** regulators (FDA DHAC, IL, CO) keep demanding "human escalation pathways". A marketplace of licensed therapists available for single sessions is exactly that pathway, so partnerships or referrals from AI companions (Headspace Ebb-style tools, Wysa Copilot-style hybrids) are plausible distribution.
- **Regulatory caution for the app's own AI:** state laws ban AI that *delivers* therapy or *detects emotions* (IL) without licensed oversight, but permit administrative and operational support. AI-drafted summaries reviewed by the treating therapist appear to fall on the permitted side. AI analysis of client journals for mood or risk could trip Illinois's emotion-detection clause. These are inferences, not legal advice.
- Woebot's and Ash's regulatory exits show that "AI therapy" as a standalone consumer business is fragile in 2025–2026. A human-therapist marketplace avoids FDA device risk, provided its AI stays administrative.

### Gaps
- Final court approval of the Character.AI settlements, and the status of the OpenAI cases as of Nov 2026, were not verified.
- No nationally representative 2026 US figure specific to ChatGPT emotional-support use was found.
- The content of Colorado HB26-1195 and California SB 1119 should be checked against official legislature pages.
- Wysa's 2025–2026 business status and Woebot's current enterprise status were not confirmed from primary sources.

## 7. Portability: carrying mental-health context to a new or covering provider

### Takeaway
It is still hard in 2026. Behavioral health was left out of the HITECH EHR incentives, interoperability is low (19% of behavioral-health facilities use HIEs), and many private-practice therapy EHRs are not ONC-certified and so lack mandated patient-access FHIR APIs. Psychotherapy notes are legally carved out of access rules. Release of information runs on fax, signed forms and 30-day clocks, and patients report waits of around two months. Federal "Kill the Clipboard" and CMS Aligned Networks efforts (2025–2026) target hospitals, payers and large EHRs, not solo therapists. No US commercial client-owned mental-health "passport" was found. UK and Australian NHS-style "mental health passports" are the nearest precedent.

### Cited Findings
- **HITECH gap:** the 2009 HITECH incentives excluded mental-health and substance-use treatment facilities, and behavioral-health providers were ineligible for Medicaid Promoting Interoperability payments ([MACPAC](https://macpac.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Integrating-Clinical-Care-through-Greater-Use-of-Electronic-Health-Records-by-Behavioral-Health-Providers.pdf)). ASPE published a December 2024 RTI report on health IT adoption in behavioral-health settings ([ASPE](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/b9f858a38ff71660528cf1e4b8df00fa/HIT-adoption-utilization-bh-settings.pdf)).
- **Exchange is weak:** only 19% of behavioral-health facilities participate in an HIE, and only 17% of hospitals send summary-of-care records to most or all of the behavioral-health providers they work with (ONC 2023 data). EHR adoption at state-government facilities is just 38% (ONC brief on SAMHSA 2024 survey) ([TechTarget on ONC data](https://www.techtarget.com/searchhealthit/feature/ONC-highlights-behavioral-health-EHR-adoption-trends-data-exchange-barriers)).
- **Certification:** the federal patient-access API criterion §170.315(g)(10) requires FHIR R4 APIs usable "without special effort", but only for *certified* health IT ([HealthIT.gov](https://www.healthit.gov/sites/default/files/page2/2020-03/APICertificationCriterion.pdf)). An EHR review site (Feb 2026) says SimplePractice is *not* ONC-certified and not on the CHPL ([EHRSource SimplePractice](https://ehrsource.com/vendors/simplepractice/)), while some directories label it ONC-certified ([Slashdot listing](https://slashdot.org/software/p/SimplePractice/)). This is unresolved. TherapyNotes is listed as ONC-certified ([EHRSource TherapyNotes](https://ehrsource.com/vendors/therapynotes/)).
- **Psychotherapy notes carve-out:** see Section 2. HIPAA psychotherapy notes kept separately are excluded from Cures Act EHI, so even perfect APIs would not carry a therapist's process notes ([CHCANYS](https://www.chcanys.org/sites/default/files/2025-04/Cures%20Act%20Basics%20for%20Providers%20%28Long%29.docx)).
- **Release-of-information friction:** HIPAA gives covered entities 30 days to act on access requests, plus one 30-day extension with written reasons ([Zur Institute](https://www.zurinstitute.com/hipaa_patient_rights_clinicalupdate.html)). State rules differ; California, for example, allows 15 business days for copies, and therapists may charge a reasonable fee to prepare a *treatment summary* instead ([CA MFT jurisprudence guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/ca-mft-juris/confidentiality-privilege-minor-consent/records-management-retention)). Counseling centers commonly send a "treatment summary" to a third party with written permission ([Drexel counseling center](https://drexel.edu/counselingandhealth/counseling-center/overview/records/)). Patient forums describe waits of about two months, repeated follow-ups, and receiving under two pages after lengthy treatment. This is anecdotal ([MyPTSD forum](https://www.myptsd.com/threads/requested-records-for-lengthy-treatment-received-less-than-2-pages.89711/page-2)).
- **Federal push (2025–2026):** CMS's voluntary Interoperability Framework (July 2025) drew about 60 pledgers, including 21 networks, 11 health systems and 7 EHR vendors. "Kill the Clipboard" commits participants to accept patient-shared data via QR codes or SMART Health Cards using FHIR bundles. Apple, Google, Samsung and CVS pledged patient-side apps ([Healthcare Finance News](https://www.healthcarefinancenews.com/news/providers-pledge-kill-clipboard-support-cms-interoperability-framework); [Medical Economics](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/white-house-and-cms-tap-amazon-apple-google-and-other-tech-giants-to-drive-patient-centered-digital-health-initiative); [CMS](https://www.cms.gov/interoperability)). Apps in the Medicare App Library must verify identity at IAL2/AAL2 via ID.me or CLEAR ([CMS requirements](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/health-technology-ecosystem/overview/submit-your-app/requirements-medicare-app-library)). Conversational-AI pledgees include OpenAI, Google, Microsoft AI and UnitedHealth ([CMS conversational AI list](https://www.cms.gov/health-tech-ecosystem/early-adopters/conversational-ai-assistants)). b.well says its CMS Aligned Network is live with real data exchange (vendor) ([b.well](https://resources.icanbwell.com/bwells-cms-aligned-network-goes-live-real-patient-data-now-exchanging-across-major-health-plans-digital-platforms-and-health-systems/)). Some apps connect via TEFCA ([CMS Polygon Health page](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/health-technology-ecosystem/overview/early-adopters-all-pledgees/health-care-keeps-up-you/polygon-health)).
- **Client-owned profiles (precedents, all outside the US):**
  - **Sheffield (UK) digital Mental Health Passport:** the person writes their history "in your own words", shares a link with supporters or clinicians, and can revoke access at any time ([Sheffield Mental Health guidance](https://www.sheffieldmentalhealth.co.uk/mental-health-passports-and-safety-plans-guidance)).
  - **NHS England children and young people's information passport:** a template to help the service user "own and communicate their story when moving between different services" ([NHS England template](https://www.england.nhs.uk/mentalhealth/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2015/10/cyp-information-passport-template.docx)).
  - **Epworth (Australia):** piloted a co-designed mental-health passport ([Epworth](https://www.epworth.org.au/newsroom/mental-health-passport-trailed-at-epworth-camberwell)).
  - **YoungMinds and Digital Health Passport:** co-creating youth mental-health features ([NHS Accelerator](https://nhsaccelerator.com/?p=2688)).
  - **No US commercial "therapy passport" product was found** in searches.
- **Chatbot data portability:** when Woebot shut down, users could download their conversation history before June 30, 2025 ([HLTH](https://hlth.com/insights/news/woebot-health-is-shutting-down-its-app-2025-04-28)). Even AI-companion histories are effectively stranded unless exported.

### Inferences
- The information a covering therapist most needs is narrative and preference data: presenting concerns, what has and hasn't helped, current meds, risk history and safety plan, triggers, preferred modality, and the regular therapist's contact. That is exactly what FHIR and USCDI pipelines do not carry well and what psychotherapy-note rules exclude. A *client-authored* profile sidesteps both the access delays and the certification gaps, because the client is the source and doesn't need a records release. The UK passports validate the concept and the "own words plus revocable link" pattern.
- A handoff from the one-off therapist back to the regular therapist currently means a signed release plus fax, email or portal message, and probably a hand-written summary. No product found standardizes this. Under HIPAA, provider-to-provider disclosure for treatment generally doesn't require client authorization (psychotherapy notes excepted). That was not verified in this research, and state law can be stricter, so legal review is needed. Routing the handoff through the client (client receives it and chooses to forward) avoids the question and keeps the client in control.
- "Kill the Clipboard" and QR/SMART Health Card intake could eventually let a passport be presented in a standard format. Behavioral-health solo practices are at the tail of adoption, so a matching app should not rely on EHR integration for v1.

### Gaps
- Apple Health Records' coverage of behavioral-health providers and of therapy content was not researched.
- 42 CFR Part 2 (substance-use records; 2024 final rule with 2026 compliance) was not researched, though it affects any profile containing SUD history.
- Whether CMS Aligned Networks met the July 2026 FHIR milestone, and whether any therapy EHR joined, was not verified.
- SimplePractice's ONC certification status remains contradictory; check the CHPL (chpl.healthit.gov).
- No US product offering a client-owned, shareable mental-health profile was found. This may be a real gap, or a search limitation since the budget ran out before trying further terms (for example, "portable mental health record app", "psychiatric advance directive app").
