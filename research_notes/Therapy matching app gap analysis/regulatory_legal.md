# US Regulatory and Legal Landscape for a One-Off-Session Telehealth Therapy Marketplace (Shared Summaries and Client Journals)

Research as of 2026-11-03. This is research, not legal advice. Compact and telehealth rules change monthly, so every status below carries an as-of date. Research limits: the session's shared web-search budget ran out partway through, and hhs.gov, telehealth.hhs.gov, psypact.org/.gov, telehealth.org and federalregister.gov returned 403 errors or certificate errors, so several items (PSYPACT's own count, the HHS OCR guidance pages, Part 2 compliance dates, and the Nevada and Utah AI laws) could not be checked against primary sources. These are listed under Gaps rather than asserted.

## 1. Licensure basics: where therapy is "practiced," and temporary-practice / continuity-of-care exceptions

### Takeaway
Telehealth is legally rendered where the client is physically located. A traveling client's own therapist is therefore generally unauthorized unless the destination state has a compact privilege, a telehealth registration, or a temporary/continuity exception. Exceptions vary a lot (from none in NY/TX/TN to 15 days, 20 days, 30 days, 90 days, 1 year, or unlimited), and most cover only an existing client. That is a gap a one-off marketplace of locally licensed or registered therapists can fill.

### Cited Findings
**Core principle**
- CCHP (Center for Connected Health Policy): "When telehealth is used, it is considered to be rendered at the physical location of the patient," so providers generally need a license in the patient's state. Some states offer telehealth-specific licenses, registrations or exceptions. State sections were last updated May–Sept 2026. — [CCHP Cross-State Licensing](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)
- APA Services: psychologists "should be licensed or legally permitted to practice" where they and the patient are located. Some states also treat the psychologist as practicing where the psychologist is. Temporary practice laws exist (example "20 days per year"); some states do not allow temporary practice, and some require a permit. Trainees generally cannot provide interstate telehealth. Page created Jan 15, 2021. — [APA Services](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/legal/technology/telehealth-different-state)
- Under PSYPACT and the Counseling Compact, the clinician must be physically located in their home member state when delivering teletherapy under a compact privilege. The privilege does not cover a clinician who is traveling or abroad. — [Person Centered Tech, Episode 616 (June 4, 2026)](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)

**State-by-state exceptions for behavioral health (from CCHP state pages, "last updated" dates in parentheses)** — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)
- **California (LMFT, LCSW, LPCC):** up to 30 consecutive days per year with *existing* clients located in CA, under conditions. The CA BBS FAQ describes it as a one-time-per-calendar-year 30-day allowance available only to persons fully licensed in another state, with the example of a client "vacationing or traveling for work in California." — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/); [CA BBS Temporary Practice FAQ](https://bbs.ca.gov/licensees/temporary_practice_allowance_faqs.pdf)
- **Colorado (psychologists, therapists, social workers, counselors, addiction counselors):** up to 20 days/year under conditions. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/). Cicero Institute reports Colorado SB 141 (2024) creates a registration pathway for out-of-state social workers and counselors starting in 2026. — [Cicero Institute (Feb 19, 2025)](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/)
- **Arizona:** out-of-state behavioral health providers without an AZ license may not provide telehealth to AZ clients for more than 90 days. CCHP also says registration is not required for providers with fewer than 10 telehealth encounters/year, or for some emergency, consultation and after-care situations. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/). Arizona's registration covers psychologists (and physicians). Counselors and social workers were excluded from the final law. There is an exemption for a provider who is the established behavioral health provider of a person temporarily in AZ (e.g., "snowbirds"). — [Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/). Arizona Board of Psychologist Examiners runs a Telehealth Registry for out-of-state psychologists. — [AZ Psychology Board Telehealth Registry](https://psychboard.az.gov/telehealth-registry)
- **District of Columbia:** visitors may be served while temporarily present, and DC residents for up to 120 days, where there is an existing practitioner-client relationship (08/13/2026).
- **Idaho:** a registration for out-of-state mental and behavioral health providers (unrestricted license in a substantially similar state, no discipline, consent to ID jurisdiction, biennial registration, no in-person care), plus a general "virtual care" exemption for existing patients temporarily in Idaho or short-term follow-up (07/06/2026).
- **Illinois (social workers):** nonresidents limited to 5 days in any month or 15 days per calendar year, with a prior established therapeutic relationship. Scheduled repeal Jan 1, 2028 (06/04/2026).
- **Wisconsin (MFTs, professional counselors, social workers):** up to 5 days in any month and 15 days total, with a previously established relationship. Psychologists need a WI license or PSYPACT (10/04/2026).
- **Washington (mental health practitioners), RCW 18.134.030:** continuity of care for an established patient temporarily located in WA. No time limit stated (08/16/2026).
- **Virginia (psychologists §54.1-3601; clinical social workers §54.1-3701):** continuity of care for up to one year from start of services with a previously established relationship (09/28/2026). LPCs and MFTs are not listed in CCHP's summary.
- **Utah:** for clients who *relocate* to Utah, psychologists get 45 days and mental health therapists/SUD counselors get 90 days, with notice to the Division. Therapists must obtain a Utah license within nine months, or sooner if serving more than one Utah client (07/31/2026).
- **Maryland (clinical professional counselors):** continuity-of-care telehealth for up to 6 months after the client relocates to MD. There is also a narrow non-renewable 6-month temporary telehealth license only for serving higher-ed students with a 6+ month relationship (06/03/2026).
- **Louisiana (LPCs):** temporary registration of up to 30 consecutive days/year, generally issued once per licensee, $100. Addiction counselors can get an out-of-state telehealth license ($250) (09/30/2026).
- **West Virginia (LPCs, MFTs):** interstate telehealth registration allows up to 30 nonconsecutive days in any 6-month period, but registrants **cannot establish new client relationships** (05/09/2026).
- **Hawaii (psychologists):** temporary permit up to 90 days/calendar year, not telehealth-specific (06/03/2026). **Mississippi (psychology):** temporary practice certificate up to 30 days per 12 months, fee ≤$300 (07/11/2026).
- **Vermont:** Telehealth Registration covers up to 10 unique patients for up to 120 consecutive days (not renewable). Telehealth License covers up to 20 unique patients over a 2-year renewable term. An interim telehealth registration explicitly includes mental health professionals. No in-person care (07/27/2026).
- **South Carolina (LPC, addiction counselor, MFT, psycho-educational specialist under §40-75-800; LISW-CP under §40-63-35):** out-of-state registration for behavioral telehealth with clients in SC, no day limit, $10 fee, no discipline in the prior 5 years, consent to jurisdiction (08/16/2026).
- **Florida:** out-of-state health care professionals register with the applicable board, with no day limit stated. They must maintain the required liability coverage, have no FL office and provide no in-person care (06/19/2026). — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/). Covers counselors, social workers, psychologists and MFTs. Cicero (an advocacy group) counted ~5,573 out-of-state counselor registrants and 2,728 out-of-state psychologist registrants as of Feb 2025. In ~2.5 years there were 57 complaints against in-state providers versus 16 against out-of-state providers. — [Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/)
- **Delaware:** an interstate registration framework for out-of-state mental health, behavioral health and social work providers. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/). Cicero says it bars practitioners who live in Social Work or Counseling Compact states. — [Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/)
- **Kentucky:** out-of-state social workers can get a temporary telehealth permit (telehealth training, written client consent). Counselors must be licensed where the client is domiciled (09/07/2026).
- **No out-of-state behavioral telehealth pathway:** New York (requires an NY license), Texas, Tennessee (only volunteer free-clinic work), Indiana (telehealth certificates ended July 1, 2024), Georgia, Kansas, Missouri (only informal uncompensated consultation and free emergency care), and New Jersey (consultation with an NJ licensee only). Connecticut's out-of-state mental health telehealth pathway expired June 30, 2025. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/). Cicero also lists North Carolina, Montana and Mississippi as having no specific interstate telehealth pathway, and says Minnesota requires an in-state license for social workers and counselors. — [Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/)
- **Alabama:** CCHP notes no AL license is needed for "irregular or infrequent" telehealth (fewer than 10 days or 10 patients/year). It is unclear from the summary whether this applies beyond physicians. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)

**Access MFT (portability, not a compact):** as of June 2026, portability legislation was enacted in 16 states (Minnesota most recently), with 3 pending including California. It leads to a full license, not temporary practice. — [Person Centered Tech (June 4, 2026)](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)

### Inferences
- **The core opportunity is confirmed.** In many high-traffic destinations (New York, Texas, Tennessee, Georgia, New Jersey, Indiana, Connecticut, Minnesota for LPC/LCSW), a traveling client's regular therapist generally has *no* legal way to keep seeing them via telehealth unless a compact privilege applies. A marketplace of therapists licensed in the destination state can lawfully serve those clients. The pitch "your therapist can't see you in Texas, but a Texas-licensed therapist can see you today" fits these states.
- **Most exceptions require an existing relationship.** That includes CA, IL, WI, WA, VA, DC, Idaho's virtual-care exemption, and WV's ban on new relationships. They help the client's *existing* therapist, not a new one-off therapist. In those states the app's one-off session competes with a lawful continuation by the regular therapist. The product could detect this and tell the client "your own therapist may legally see you here for up to N days." That builds trust and creates a referral loop with primary therapists.
- **Registration states widen supply for new one-off clients** without a full local license: Florida, South Carolina, Idaho, Vermont (capped at 10–20 patients), Delaware, Arizona (psychologists), and Colorado (social workers and counselors, if SB 141 is implemented). Florida is a major travel destination with an uncapped registration, which makes it an early launch market.
- **A "jurisdiction engine" is a product requirement and a moat.** It needs session-time client location capture, a rules table by state × license type × relationship status × day count, and per-therapist day counters (e.g., CA 30 consecutive days/yr, CO 20 days/yr, IL/WI 5 days/month and 15/yr). No one-size rule exists, and the rules change often (CT lapsed in 2025, IN ended in 2024, and IL's SW provision repeals in 2028).
- **Arizona's possible "<10 encounters/year" exemption** and Alabama's "irregular or infrequent" exemption could, if they apply to behavioral health professions, suit low-volume one-off sessions. They need verification before relying on them.

### Gaps
- I could not find a single authoritative 50-state table of temporary-practice rules specific to *psychologists, LPCs, LCSWs and LMFTs*. CCHP is the closest, but the first 100k characters (AL–DE) were only partly summarized, and several state sections were truncated (Maine, New Mexico, Tennessee). ASPPB's temporary-practice chart and AAMFT/ACA state charts were not retrieved.
- A New Jersey limit ("10 consecutive business days or 15 business days in any 90-day period") appeared only in a fragmentary older CA BBS comparison chart. CCHP's 10/06/2026 NJ entry lists only consultation and supervision exemptions, so treat NJ's temporary practice as unverified.
- Whether Arizona's "<10 encounters" registration exemption and Alabama's "irregular/infrequent" exemption cover counselors, social workers and MFTs is unconfirmed.
- Florida's "15 days per year" claim (from a secondary search snippet) conflicts with CCHP's "no day limit stated" for registration. I favored CCHP.

## 2. Interstate compacts as of November 2026

### Takeaway
PSYPACT is the only mature compact (~43 effective states as of mid-2026). The Counseling Compact is now issuing privileges in about 11 states (as of late Oct 2026), but most of its 40 member jurisdictions are not live. The Social Work Compact (32–35 states) has issued no multistate licenses. There is no MFT compact. Compacts help a home-based therapist serve a client in another compact state, but they do not cover therapists who are themselves traveling or abroad.

### Cited Findings
**PSYPACT (psychologists)**
- As of June 4, 2026: enacted in 45 states, effective in 43. — [Person Centered Tech (June 4, 2026)](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)
- Nebraska psychology board minutes (Jan 2026) report 43 states joined, with Montana most recent. — [Nebraska DHHS minutes, Jan 30, 2026](https://dhhs.ne.gov/licensure/LU%20Agendas%20Minutes/013026psychminutes.pdf)
- A July 2026 industry summary calls PSYPACT "operational" across 40+ states and territories. It notes the E.Passport supports telepsychology and the Interjurisdictional Practice Certificate (IPC) supports temporary in-person services. — [Triad (July 6, 2026)](https://b2b.triadhq.com/blog/behavioral-health-licensure-compacts-2026)
- Telepsychology under PSYPACT requires an E.Passport, and temporary in-person practice requires an IPC. Both psychologist and patient must be in PSYPACT states. If the other state is not in PSYPACT, its own temporary practice law applies. — [APA Services](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/legal/technology/telehealth-different-state)
- A search summary of a Triad post reported Alaska passed PSYPACT in June 2026 and Iowa in July 2026, adding that enactment alone does not allow immediate practice. This could not be verified, and the Iowa claim may be wrong if Iowa joined earlier. — [Triad](https://www.triadhq.com/blog/telehealth-across-state-lines-2026)

**Counseling Compact (LPCs)**
- Membership: 39 states plus DC (40 jurisdictions). — [Counseling Compact map](https://counselingcompact.gov/map/)
- Live (privileges being issued): the homepage lists **Arizona, Arkansas, Georgia, Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Missouri, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee and Wyoming** (11 states). It also says 29 more states and DC are working through implementation, and announces Missouri's go-live on Oct 19, 2026. — [counselingcompact.gov (accessed Nov 2026)](https://counselingcompact.gov/). The /map/ page still says "live for licensees in Arizona, Minnesota, and Ohio" and is stale. — [Counseling Compact map](https://counselingcompact.gov/map/)
- Timeline: first live Sept 30, 2025 in AZ and MN. As of June 4, 2026, five states were live (AZ, MN, GA, LA, OH), Georgia went live June 2, 2026, and no timeline had been set for the other enacted states. — [Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/). Ohio began issuing privileges Jan 5, 2026 (the 3rd state). — [ACA](https://www.counseling.org/advocacy/counseling-compact/2026/01/05/ohio-becomes-the-3rd-state-to-grant-counseling-compact-privileges); [Counseling Compact news](https://counselingcompact.gov/counseling-compact-goes-live-in-ohio/). Arkansas became the 7th state to issue privileges (reported July 30, 2026). — [Counseling Compact news](https://counselingcompact.gov/news/)
- Georgia: Associate Professional Counselors are not eligible. A privilege expires with the home-state license. — [Georgia SOS](https://sos.ga.gov/page/counseling-compact)
- Privileges are per state (e.g., an Arizona LPC applies for a Minnesota privilege). — [Counseling Compact map](https://counselingcompact.gov/map/)

**Social Work Licensure Compact**
- The compact has reached activation, but "multistate licenses are not yet being issued." Implementation is described as 12–24 months. — [swcompact.org](https://swcompact.org/)
- 32 states enacted (up from 23 a year earlier), 4 pending, with an estimated 9–12 months to go-live as of end of May 2026. — [Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/). NASW-NC reports 35 states as of July 2026, 3 pending, and no multistate licenses issued yet. — [NASW North Carolina](https://www.naswnc.org/page/SWCompact). As of mid-2026, no multistate privileges had been issued, the data system and rules were still being built, and no fee schedule had been finalized. — [Triad](https://b2b.triadhq.com/blog/behavioral-health-licensure-compacts-2026)
- Unlike PSYPACT and the Counseling Compact, practitioners expect the SW compact *not* to require the clinician to be physically in their home state. — [Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)

**MFTs**
- There is no dedicated MFT compact. LMFTs are covered by neither the Counseling nor the SW compact. AAMFT's Access MFT portability model is the alternative (16 states as of June 2026). — [Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)

**Nurse Licensure Compact / APRN Compact (psychiatric NPs)**
- NLC: "43 jurisdictions are currently part of the NLC" (no as-of date shown). — [nursecompact.com](https://www.nursecompact.com/). NCSBN presents the APRN Compact as separate from the NLC, so the NLC multistate RN license does not itself confer APRN practice authority. — [NCSBN Compacts](https://www.ncsbn.org/compacts)

### Inferences
- **Compacts do not solve the traveling-client problem.** Privileges are bought state by state, so a therapist rarely holds privileges for wherever the client happens to travel. Counseling Compact coverage is live only between ~11 states. SW and MFT have no operational interstate mechanism. Psychologists are the exception: a PSYPACT E.Passport holder can see an existing or new client in ~43 states, so psychologists with E.Passports are the highest-value supply for a one-off travel marketplace.
- **Compacts do not cover a therapist who travels.** PSYPACT and the Counseling Compact require the clinician to be in their home state. A "therapist on vacation" supply model under compact authority won't work.
- **Product opportunity:** ingest each therapist's licenses, compact privileges (PSYPACT E.Passport, Counseling Compact privilege list) and registrations (FL, SC, ID, VT, DE), then match only on lawful authorization for the client's *current* location. Compact data may be verifiable through commission data systems, but this was not confirmed.

### Gaps
- PSYPACT's official site (psypact.org) failed with an expired certificate and psypact.gov returned 403, so the exact Nov 2026 member and effective count is unverified (best estimate 43 effective / 45 enacted as of June 2026). The IPC's day limit (commonly cited as 30 days/year in-person) was not confirmed in a retrieved source.
- Counseling Compact: the homepage lists Oklahoma, Tennessee and Wyoming as live, but their go-live dates were not confirmed. The homepage's Missouri entry was ambiguous about whether its Oct 19, 2026 go-live had happened.
- SW Compact: the exact state list and any announced first-issuance date for late 2026/2027 were not found.
- APRN Compact: the number of enacting states and whether it has reached the 7-state activation threshold were not found (aprncompact.com gave no figures).

## 3. International: US client abroad, foreign visitor in the US

### Takeaway
Licensing follows the client's location in both directions. A US therapist serving a US client who is abroad must be legally permitted under the *foreign* country's law, and US licensing boards and compacts provide no cover. A foreign visitor physically in a US state can be served lawfully by a therapist licensed in that state. This asymmetry favors serving inbound visitors and treating "US client abroad" sessions as high risk.

### Cited Findings
- APA: the psychologist should be licensed or legally permitted where both the psychologist and the patient are located, "including other countries." Foreign licensing rules can be hard to determine, so check with the national psychology association. Clinical factors to weigh include time zones, cultural issues, availability of local therapists, crisis and emergency resources, and foreign patient data-privacy requirements. APA recommends consulting the professional liability carrier. — [APA Services](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/legal/technology/telehealth-different-state)
- Compact privileges do not cover care delivered while the clinician is abroad. — [Person Centered Tech](https://personcenteredtech.com/2026/06/04/episode-616-licensure-compacts-portability-update/)
- US states locate telehealth at the patient's physical location. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)
- The ACA Code of Ethics has a dedicated distance-counseling section, including H.1.b on laws of both the practicing location and the client's place of residence, H.2.a on informed consent elements (emergency procedures, time zones, cultural/language, insurance), and H.3 on client verification. Only the section headings could be confirmed from the retrieved PDF. — [ACA Code of Ethics (2014)](https://www.counseling.org/docs/default-source/default-document-library/ethics/2014-aca-code-of-ethics.pdf)

### Inferences
- **US client abroad:** legally unsettled and country-specific. Many countries don't regulate "counseling" as a protected title, while others regulate psychotherapy strictly. Foreign data-protection law (e.g., GDPR for clients in the EU) may also apply. A new product should either (a) geofence sessions to US jurisdictions, or (b) offer abroad sessions only with explicit risk disclosures, a local crisis plan and carrier confirmation. Option (a) is the safer MVP.
- **Foreign visitor in the US:** fully lawful with a state-licensed therapist. It is an underserved niche (tourists, students, business travelers) that maps onto the "one-off while traveling" concept.
- Emergency planning is harder abroad (no 988; different emergency numbers), which raises duty-of-care risk.

### Gaps
- No malpractice insurer policy language was retrieved (HPSO, CPH, The Trust, American Professional Agency) on (a) territorial limits for clients abroad, or (b) coverage for single-session or one-off telehealth. This needs direct verification. Many policies are believed to require suits to be brought in the US, but that is unverified here.
- No AAMFT, ASPPB or ASWB position on clients abroad was retrieved.

## 4. Telehealth practice requirements and federal flexibilities (Medicare, DEA)

### Takeaway
Boards' telehealth rules require licensure where the client is, plus telehealth-specific consent, training and verification steps (e.g., Louisiana and Kentucky). Professional codes require verifying identity and location and setting emergency procedures. Medicare's behavioral-health in-person requirement is waived through Dec 31, 2027 under the FY2026 appropriations law. A cash-pay one-off model largely avoids payer rules.

### Cited Findings
- Louisiana LPC teletherapy requires board approval, licensure where the client is located, and 3 hours of training. Kentucky's out-of-state SW temporary telehealth permit requires telehealth training, compliance with KY telehealth law and written client consent. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)
- ACA Code Section H covers informed consent (including emergency procedures), client verification, and records for distance counseling (headings confirmed only). — [ACA Code of Ethics](https://www.counseling.org/docs/default-source/default-document-library/ethics/2014-aca-code-of-ethics.pdf)
- **Medicare:** the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (H.R. 7148), signed Feb 3, 2026, extended Medicare telehealth flexibilities through Dec 31, 2027. — [Medical Economics (Feb 10, 2026)](https://medicaleconomics.com/view/what-changed-in-medicare-telehealth-under-the-2026-spending-bill); [PYA](https://www.pyapc.com/insights/medicare-extends-telehealth-coverage-through-2027-updates-and-reminders-for-providers/); [Greenbaum Rowe](https://www.greenbaumlaw.com/insights-alerts-Medicare-Telehealth-Flexibilities-Extended-Through-2027-Telehealth-Gets-a-Two-Year-Lifeline-in-Fiscal-Year-2026-Spending-Package.html)
- The behavioral-health in-person requirement (an in-person visit within 6 months before the first mental-health telehealth visit, then every 12 months) is waived through 2027 and returns Jan 1, 2028. Patients established before then would need only the annual visit. — [telehealth.org](https://telehealth.org/news/federal-telehealth-policy-in-2026-what-the-medicare-extensions-mean/); [Baker Donelson](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/medicare-telehealth-waivers-extended-for-several-more-years)
- Before the extension, the waiver had briefly been set to lapse Jan 30, 2026, after the shutdown-ending CR. — [telehealth.org](https://telehealth.org/blog/shutdown-ends-medicare-telehealth-flexibilities-extended-through-january-30-2026/)
- Sources conflict on which elements are permanent. Some say home as an originating site for behavioral health and FQHC/RHC distant-site status are permanent, and others call the extension temporary. — [Medical Economics](https://medicaleconomics.com/view/what-changed-in-medicare-telehealth-under-the-2026-spending-bill); [Baker Donelson](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/medicare-telehealth-waivers-extended-for-several-more-years)
- **DEA (only relevant if prescribers join):** the fourth temporary extension allows tele-prescribing of Schedule II–V drugs without an in-person evaluation through Dec 31, 2026. The buprenorphine telemedicine final rule took effect Dec 31, 2025. The special registration rule (proposed Jan 2025) was not finalized as of the sources. — [McDermott+](https://www.mcdermottplus.com/insights/dea-extends-telemedicine-flexibilities-for-controlled-substance-prescribing-for-2026/); [Behavioral Health Business (Dec 31, 2025)](https://bhbusiness.com/2025/12/31/telehealth-prescribing-flexibilities-avoid-lapse-dea-extends-rule-through-2026/)

### Inferences
- **Product must-haves:**
  - Location attestation and capture at *each* session start, not only at signup. A traveling client's state changes, and that determines which therapists are lawful.
  - Identity verification.
  - Telehealth informed consent covering technology risks, privacy, emergency procedures, and the one-off nature of the session.
  - An emergency plan captured per session: current physical address, a local emergency contact, and local crisis resources.
  - Therapist attestations of state-specific telehealth training where required (LA, KY).
- **Policy and payer exposure:** the one-off travel concept fits cash pay or out-of-network superbills. The DEA cliff at end of 2026 and Medicare's 2028 in-person rule are reasons to exclude prescribing and Medicare from the MVP.

### Gaps
- Audio-only and text-based therapy rules were not verified this session. Questions include whether boards allow asynchronous/text therapy, Medicare's permanent audio-only allowance for home mental health services, and state parity laws.
- No compiled count of states whose boards require location verification, emergency plans or telehealth consent for mental health professions was retrieved. The CCHP consent and professional-board pages returned 404, and telehealth.hhs.gov returned 403.
- Whether a DEA extension beyond Dec 31, 2026 or a special-registration final rule has issued was not checked after early 2026.

## 5. Privacy: HIPAA, psychotherapy notes, information blocking, Part 2, FTC, state health-data laws, minors

### Takeaway
How the platform is regulated depends on structure. If it hosts records on behalf of therapist covered entities, it is a HIPAA business associate. If it runs consumer-facing features such as client journals not held for a provider, the FTC Act, the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (HBNR) and state consumer-health-data laws like Washington's MHMDA apply. In practice it will be both. HIPAA "psychotherapy notes" (separate process notes) are excluded from patient access and from information-blocking rules. A client-facing summary, by contrast, sits in the designated record set and is patient-accessible. The FTC's BetterHelp, GoodRx and Premom cases make ad-tech sharing of health data without express consent the top enforcement risk.

### Cited Findings
**HIPAA scope**
- HIPAA covers covered entities (health plans, providers that conduct standard electronic transactions, clearinghouses) and partly business associates under a BAA. The FTC Act applies to companies "that aren't required to comply with HIPAA." The HBNR covers PHR vendors, PHR-related entities and service providers not covered by HIPAA. Health information is broad and includes browsing, location and purchase data that support a health inference. Avoid claiming to be "HIPAA Compliant." (FTC guidance, Aug 2024) — [FTC Business Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)

**Psychotherapy notes (45 CFR 164.501)**
- Psychotherapy notes are "notes recorded (in any medium) by a health care provider who is a mental health professional documenting or analyzing the contents of conversation during a private counseling session or a group, joint, or family counseling session and that are separated from the rest of the individual's medical record." The definition excludes "medication prescription and monitoring, counseling session start and stop times, the modalities and frequencies of treatment furnished, results of clinical tests, and any summary of … Diagnosis, functional status, the treatment plan, symptoms, prognosis, and progress to date." — [45 CFR 164.501 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.501)
- The designated record set includes provider medical and billing records and records "used, in whole or in part, by or for the covered entity to make decisions about individuals." — [45 CFR 164.501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.501)

**Patient access (45 CFR 164.524)**
- The right of access excludes "(i) Psychotherapy notes," as an unreviewable denial. Other records can be withheld only on reviewable grounds, e.g., a licensed professional determines access "is reasonably likely to endanger the life or physical safety of the individual or another person," with review by a different designated licensed professional. Requests must be acted on within 30 days, with one 30-day extension. — [45 CFR 164.524](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.524)

**Information blocking (21st Century Cures Act)**
- Any note meeting the HIPAA psychotherapy-notes definition is excluded from "electronic health information." This turns on content, not the author's license type. — [ASTP/ONC FAQ](https://healthit.gov/faq/does-electronic-health-information-definitions-exclusion-psychotherapy-notes-apply-notes/)
- Other mental-health progress notes do not get the same treatment. Notes are protected only if kept separate, and content such as diagnoses, symptoms and treatment plans cannot be blocked on that basis. — [Qualifacts](https://www.qualifacts.com/resources/mental-health-providers-information-blocking/)
- **Preventing Harm exception (45 CFR 171.201):** it requires a "reasonable belief that the practice will substantially reduce a risk of harm," be "no broader than necessary," and rest on an individualized determination by a licensed professional (or on corrupt/erroneous data). The harm must be of a type that would justify a HIPAA access denial. Patients' review rights must be honored. — [45 CFR 171.201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/171.201)

**42 CFR Part 2 (SUD records)**
- Part 2 applies to records identifying a patient as having a SUD that come from a *federally assisted* "part 2 program" for SUD diagnosis, treatment or referral. That includes private practitioners who "hold themselves out as providing" SUD services. Federal assistance includes Medicare participation, federal licensing or registration (e.g., DEA for SUD treatment), federal funds, and IRS tax-exempt status. A non-Part 2 provider recording SUD information in its own record does not make that record Part 2. Records received under a single TPO consent need not be segregated. Use of records in proceedings against the patient is restricted. — [42 CFR 2.12 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/2.12)

**FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (2024 update)**
- Finalized Apr 26, 2024, the update clarifies that the rule covers health apps not covered by HIPAA. It adds definitions for "covered health care provider" and "health care services or supplies," clarifies what drawing from "multiple sources" means, and defines a breach to include *unauthorized disclosure* (not just hacking). Notice is due without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days. For 500+ individuals, the FTC must be notified at the same time as consumers. Effective 60 days after Federal Register publication. — [FTC Press Release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule); [FTC Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)

**FTC and DOJ enforcement**
- BetterHelp, GoodRx and Premom are cited by the FTC as showing that disclosing health information for advertising without affirmative express consent "may be an unfair practice." — [FTC Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)
- BetterHelp: the FTC alleged (March 2023) that it shared emails, IP addresses and health-questionnaire answers with Facebook, Snapchat, Criteo and Pinterest despite privacy promises. The order was finalized July 2023 with $7.8M in consumer refunds and a ban on sharing health data for advertising. Refunds began by May 2024. — [Wikipedia: BetterHelp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BetterHelp)
- Cerebral: agreed in Nov 2024 to pay $3.65M to resolve a DOJ Controlled Substances Act probe. — [Wikipedia: Cerebral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_(company))

**State consumer health data laws**
- Washington MHMDA (HB 1155): Section 10 applied to all persons from July 23, 2023. Regulated entities had to comply from Mar 31, 2024, small businesses from June 30, 2024. Consumer health data includes past, present or future mental health status and *inferences* from non-health data. Selling requires a signed authorization kept for 6 years. Any violation is a per se violation of the WA Consumer Protection Act, enforced by the AG with a **private right of action**. The law reaches out-of-state entities targeting WA consumers. — [WA Attorney General](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy)

### Inferences
- **Recommended architecture (two-tier notes):**
  - (a) Optional private "process notes" that are kept separate and never shared. They count as HIPAA psychotherapy notes, are excluded from access and EHI, and need specific authorization for most disclosures.
  - (b) A client-facing "session summary" that by definition is *not* a psychotherapy note, because it summarizes progress, plan or symptoms. It is part of the designated record set, so the client has an access right anyway.
  - Sharing summaries is therefore legally low-friction and aligned with Cures Act openness. The legal risk lies in *what* is written, since summaries are discoverable and accessible, not in sharing them.
- **One-off sessions with summaries sent to the client's regular therapist:** this is a HIPAA disclosure for treatment purposes and is generally allowed between providers. Best practice, and required under stricter state laws and for any Part 2 content, is explicit client consent per share. This "continuity relay" from the one-off therapist back to the primary therapist could be a differentiator.
- **Platform status:** where therapists are covered entities (many cash-only therapists may not be, if they never bill electronically), the app hosting video, notes and summaries for them is likely a business associate and needs BAAs. Journals the client keeps for themselves are consumer data under the FTC HBNR (the app is likely a "PHR vendor" when it draws data from multiple sources, such as client entries plus therapist summaries) and WA MHMDA-type laws. Design for both: no ad pixels or third-party trackers on any health-adjacent page, opt-in consent for every sharing flow, and breach processes meeting both HIPAA and HBNR timelines.
- **Part 2** is unlikely to apply unless the platform or its therapists hold themselves out as SUD treatment and are federally assisted (e.g., Medicare-enrolled). Avoid marketing "addiction treatment" in the MVP.

### Gaps
- HHS OCR's mental health and HIPAA guidance and its health-app scenarios pages returned 403. HHS's position that providers may voluntarily share psychotherapy notes with the patient was not re-verified.
- The 2024 Part 2 final rule's effective date (Apr 16, 2024) and compliance date (Feb 16, 2026) are from background knowledge. They were not verified because federalregister.gov blocked access.
- FTC primary press releases on BetterHelp, Cerebral (FTC, April 2024, about $7M including pixel-tracking claims) and Monument (April 2024, $2.5M penalty suspended) were not retrieved. Treat the Cerebral FTC and Monument figures as background, not verified.
- Nevada SB 370 (consumer health data, effective Mar 31, 2024), the 2023 Connecticut CTDPA consumer-health-data amendments, the New York Health Information Privacy Act's status, and Maryland's Online Data Privacy Act were not verified in this session.
- The HIPAA Security Rule NPRM (Jan 2025) status as of Nov 2026 was not checked.
- **Minors' consent:** state ages of consent for outpatient mental health care vary widely (commonly 12–16 in states that allow it). No reliable compilation was retrieved, so I recommend the MVP be 18+ only.

## 6. Client journals shared with therapists: clinical record status and duty to monitor

### Takeaway
Once a therapist reviews journal entries and uses them in treatment decisions, they very likely fall within HIPAA's "designated record set." No retrieved statute or regulation imposes a duty on therapists to monitor shared journals in real time. The liability risk comes from implied expectations and from actual knowledge combined with duty-to-protect law, so product design must set explicit expectations.

### Cited Findings
- The designated record set includes records "used, in whole or in part, by or for the covered entity to make decisions about individuals," and a record is any information including PHI "maintained, collected, used, or disseminated by or for a covered entity." — [45 CFR 164.501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.501)
- Psychotherapy notes must be written *by the mental health professional* documenting or analyzing session conversation. Client-authored content does not fit that definition. — [45 CFR 164.501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.501)
- Duty-to-warn/protect law varies by state (see Section 8): 29 states mandatory, 16 permissive. — [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/health/mental-health-professionals-duty-to-warn)
- New 2026 chatbot laws require crisis detection in some contexts (CO, GA, ID, WA, OR, RI, mostly effective 2027). These target AI chatbots, not human-reviewed journals. — [Manatt Health AI Policy Tracker (July 30, 2026)](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)

### Inferences
- Shared journal entries that a therapist reads become part of that therapist's clinical record: client-accessible, subject to retention rules and discoverable. They cannot be shielded as psychotherapy notes.
- **Design to limit unintended duties:**
  - (a) A "push" model (client chooses specific entries to share, ideally before a session) rather than a continuous "pull" feed.
  - (b) Clear, repeated disclosure that shared entries are *not monitored* between sessions, with 988 and local crisis resources shown in the journal UI.
  - (c) Timestamped therapist acknowledgment of what was read and when.
  - (d) For one-off therapists, share access that expires after the session.
  - (e) If any automated risk-keyword detection is added, it may itself create an expectation or duty to act, and AI-based detection may trigger state chatbot laws. Treat it as a deliberate decision.
- Journals kept privately by the client (never shared) stay consumer data under FTC/HBNR and state health-data laws, not HIPAA. The app needs strong encryption and no ad-tech use.

### Gaps
- No case law or board guidance was retrieved on clinician liability for unreviewed patient-generated data (journals, messages or PGHD) in mental health. The ONC PGHD white paper URL returned 404. This area of law is unsettled and should be flagged as such.
- Whether reading a shared journal between sessions creates a "therapeutic relationship" for a one-off therapist after the session ends is not addressed in any retrieved source.

## 7. AI in mental health: state laws, FDA activity, and implications for AI-generated session summaries

### Takeaway
Illinois (2025) banned AI from providing therapy or making therapeutic decisions. In 2026, Colorado, Maine, Vermont and Rhode Island enacted laws allowing licensed professionals to use AI only for administrative or supplementary support. Several require **written notice and consent before AI records or transcribes a session**. AI-drafted session summaries are permissible in most places if a licensed therapist reviews and owns them and the client consents, but consent-before-recording is becoming a multistate norm.

### Cited Findings
- **Illinois Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act (HB 1806):** signed ~Aug 1, 2025 (IDFPR release dated Aug 4, 2025). It bars using AI to provide mental health services or make therapeutic decisions and permits AI for administrative tasks and supplementary support for licensed professionals. — [IDFPR press release](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/news/2025/gov-pritzker-signs-state-leg-prohibiting-ai-therapy-in-il.html). Manatt lists an Illinois behavioral-health AI framework effective 8/1/2025 that "other states have drawn on," plus a 2025 Illinois law barring chatbots from representing themselves as licensed providers. — [Manatt (July 30, 2026)](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)
- **2026 enactments** — all from [Manatt Health AI Policy Tracker (as of July 30, 2026)](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker):
  - Colorado (eff. 8/12/2026): professionals may use AI only for administrative or supplementary support and remain responsible. AI may not make independent therapeutic decisions or interact directly with clients. Written notice and written consent are required before AI records or transcribes a session.
  - Maine (eff. 7/29/2026): same limits, plus written notice of how session data is stored, retained, *used for training* and deleted, and consent.
  - Vermont (eff. 6/17/2026): HIPAA-compliant AI may be used within scope with professional review and approval. Entities may not offer mental health services, including through AI, unless a licensed professional provides them (or under IRB research).
  - Rhode Island (eff. 6/22/2026): written notice and consent before using AI companion-type tools in recorded or transcribed sessions. Rhode Island and Louisiana (eff. 8/1/2026) also require notice when AI records or documents a visit, and Louisiana's rule is not mental-health-specific.
  - Chatbot laws: 88 chatbot bills introduced in 2026, and 11 states enacted 12 (the tracker elsewhere says 7, an internal inconsistency). Six prohibit chatbots from representing themselves as mental health providers (e.g., HI SB 3001 eff. 7/14/2026, IA SF 2417 eff. 7/1/2027, NE LB 525). Crisis-detection and minor safeguards apply in CO (1/1/2027), GA (7/1/2027), ID (7/1/2027), WA, OR and RI (1/1/2027). New York's "Safe by Design Act" requires age assurance for AI companions from 1/1/2027.
  - Texas: a board rule (not statute) for behavior-analysis licensees bars AI as the sole basis for treatment decisions and requires personal review and documentation.
  - California: a bill restricting AI in therapy (including consent for AI recording or triage) was engrossed but not enacted as of the tracker date.
  - Pennsylvania sued Character Technologies (May 2026) under its Medical Practice Act, alleging a chatbot presented itself as a licensed psychiatrist. The case is undecided.
  - 27 states introduced 46 bills on AI in health care settings, and six states enacted seven, with a heavy focus on behavioral health.
- **Federal:** EO 14365 "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" (Dec 2025) directs DOJ to challenge burdensome state AI laws and Commerce to evaluate them (due Mar 11, 2026, not published as of July 30, 2026). — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker). The EO exempts state laws on child safety among others. — [Wikipedia: AI regulation in the US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_artificial_intelligence_in_the_United_States). A June 4, 2026 congressional discussion draft would preempt state AI *development* laws for 3 years but not laws regulating AI *use*. — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)
- **FDA:** the Digital Health Advisory Committee met Nov 6, 2025 on "generative AI-enabled digital mental health medical devices" (benefits, risks, premarket evidence, postmarket monitoring) and made recommendations. No 2026 meetings are listed (page updated 12/16/2025). — [FDA DHAC](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/fda-digital-health-advisory-committee)

### Inferences
- **AI-generated session summaries are viable under the "administrative/supplementary support" carve-out** if:
  - (a) the therapist reviews, edits and approves every summary before it reaches the client;
  - (b) written notice and consent for any recording or transcription is collected per client, with a state-aware consent flow for CO, ME, RI, LA and likely IL;
  - (c) the platform discloses retention and whether session data trains models (Maine requires this, so "no training on session data" is a simple, marketable default);
  - (d) the AI never chats with clients therapeutically.
- **Any AI feature that talks to clients** (a journal "reflection" bot or between-session chatbot) risks the Illinois ban, misrepresentation bans in six or more states, and crisis-detection duties from 2027. Keep the MVP free of client-facing AI or limit it to non-therapeutic utilities.
- **Federal preemption is uncertain.** The EO targets state AI laws, but practice-of-profession and use laws, and child-safety laws, look unlikely to be preempted soon. Build to the strictest state rules.
- **Vermont's rule that only licensed professionals may offer mental health services** reinforces that the platform must present itself as connecting clients to licensed therapists, not as providing therapy itself.

### Gaps
- Nevada AB 406 (2025; reportedly bans AI systems from providing or representing professional mental health care, with civil penalties) and Utah HB 452 (2025; mental-health chatbot disclosures, limits on selling health data, and an affirmative defense) could not be verified. The Utah legislature page gave no text and searches were exhausted. California SB 243 (companion chatbots, 2025) and AB 489 (AI implying licensure) were also not verified.
- Illinois WOPR Act specifics (consent for recording/transcription, penalties of up to $10,000 per violation enforced by IDFPR) are from background knowledge and were not confirmed in the retrieved IDFPR release.
- No FDA guidance specific to AI therapy summaries was found. Summaries for clinician documentation are likely not a device, but this was not confirmed.

## 8. Platform liability: corporate practice, malpractice, duty to warn, crisis response, and whether a marketplace "practices" therapy

### Takeaway
The safest posture is a technology marketplace where licensed therapists (as independent practitioners or through a professional entity) deliver and own all clinical services, while the platform handles matching, scheduling, payments and records infrastructure. Duty-to-warn law varies by state (29 mandatory, 16 permissive, 3 none). The platform must route each session's emergency plan and the therapist's state duties to the *client's location state*. States are increasingly using professional-practice acts against tech platforms (Vermont 2026 law; Pennsylvania v. Character Technologies).

### Cited Findings
- **Duty to warn/protect** (NCSL, updated Mar 16, 2022):
  - Mandatory (29): AL, AZ, CA, CO, DE, ID, IN, IA, KY, LA, MD, MA, MI, MN, MO, MT, NE, NV, NH, NJ, NY, OH, PA, TN, UT, VT, VA, WA, WI.
  - Permissive (16): AK, AR, CT, FL, HI, KS, MS, NM, OK, OR, RI, SC, SD, TX, WV, WY.
  - No provision (3): ME, NC, ND.
  - Mixed/unclear: GA, IL.
  - (Counts are from reading NCSL's chart.) — [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/health/mental-health-professionals-duty-to-warn)
- Vermont (eff. 6/17/2026): entities may not offer mental health services, including through AI, unless a licensed professional provides them. — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)
- Pennsylvania sued Character Technologies in May 2026 under its Medical Practice Act over a chatbot presenting as a licensed psychiatrist. — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/health-highlights/manatt-health-health-ai-policy-tracker)
- Florida's out-of-state telehealth registration requires maintaining required professional liability coverage. — [CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/topic/cross-state-licensing-professional-requirements/)
- APA advises psychologists practicing interstate to consult their professional liability carrier. — [APA Services](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/legal/technology/telehealth-different-state)
- BetterHelp's model includes verifying provider licensure and a clinician-reviewed case-study exam. No licensing enforcement against the platform is described. — [Wikipedia: BetterHelp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BetterHelp)
- In Florida, out-of-state telehealth providers drew 16 complaints versus 57 for in-state providers over ~2.5 years (advocacy-group data). — [Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/states-need-to-update-telehealth-laws-to-improve-access-to-mental-health-services/)

### Inferences
- **"Practicing" therapy:** a pure matching marketplace that does not direct clinical judgment is generally not practicing. Risk rises if the platform sets treatment protocols, controls clinical content (including AI-generated content), or markets itself as "providing therapy." Copy should say "connect with a licensed therapist," and clinical decisions must sit with the therapist.
- **Corporate practice:** corporate-practice-of-medicine doctrines mostly target physicians (psychiatry and prescribing). Some states also restrict lay ownership of psychology or counseling professional corporations. A non-prescribing therapist marketplace using independent-contractor licensed professionals generally faces lower CPOM risk than psychiatric telehealth, but this needs state-by-state review (no source retrieved; see Gaps).
- **One-off-session duty of care:** a single session still creates a full therapist-client relationship, with duties of assessment, emergency planning, documentation, record retention, termination/referral and duty to warn under the *client-location* state's law. The product should:
  - (a) collect a current physical address and local emergency contact before each session;
  - (b) show the therapist the applicable state's duty-to-warn regime and mandated-reporting rules;
  - (c) offer a structured "closing" that includes referral back to the client's regular therapist, 988 and local crisis resources;
  - (d) provide a summary hand-off.
- **Malpractice:** require each therapist to carry individual coverage that explicitly covers telehealth in every state where they see clients. Consider a platform-level E&O or umbrella policy and verify that policies don't exclude single-session or episodic care.
- **Crisis response:** the platform needs an escalation runbook (a therapist's in-session crisis protocol, local emergency services lookup by client location, 988). For international clients this is much harder, which is another reason to geofence to the US at launch.

### Gaps
- No primary or law-firm source was retrieved on corporate-practice or professional-entity rules for psychologists, counselors, social workers and MFTs. The Wikipedia CPOM page returned 404, and searches were exhausted. 2025 developments (California and Oregon restrictions on MSO/private-equity control of medical practices) were not verified.
- No insurer documents on coverage for one-off sessions, marketplace models or clients abroad were retrieved.
- NCSL's duty-to-warn chart dates to Mar 2022, so post-2022 changes are not captured.
- No sources were retrieved on whether platforms are liable under negligent-selection or credentialing theories for therapist misconduct, or how Section 230 applies to therapist marketplaces. Treat this as unsettled.
- No source was retrieved on independent-contractor classification risk for therapists on marketplaces (state ABC tests, e.g., California).
