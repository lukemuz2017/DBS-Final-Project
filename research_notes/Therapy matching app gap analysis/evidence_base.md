## 1. Single-session therapy and interventions: effect sizes, durability, who benefits, and evidence in adults

### Takeaway
Single-session interventions (SSIs) produce small average benefits: pooled SMD about −0.25 across 415 trials, and g = 0.32 in youth. The benefits are largest right after the session and against no-treatment controls. They shrink sharply against active controls and over time. The best adult evidence is mixed to weak. A 2026 megastudy of 7,505 US adults found effects of d ≤ 0.37 right after the session, but only two of 12 SSIs still beat control at 4 weeks (d = 0.14 to 0.15), and none did after correcting for multiple comparisons. A separate well-powered adult trial was null. Data from human-delivered walk-in single-session therapy (Ontario) show faster short-term relief than a waitlist, but no difference by 10 weeks.

### Cited Findings

**Meta-analytic evidence**
- Schleider & Weisz (2017, *JAACAP*), the founding meta-analysis: 50 RCTs with 10,508 youths. Overall post-intervention Hedges g = 0.32, meaning a 58% probability that an SSI recipient fares better than a control. By problem: anxiety 0.56, conduct problems 0.54, depression 0.21 (not significant), substance use 0.08. Effects were smaller at follow-ups beyond 13 weeks. Self-administered and therapist-administered SSIs did not differ significantly. — [Harvard DASH record](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/41292903); [MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/content/little-treatments-show-big-promise-youth-psychiatric-problems)
- In that youth meta-analysis, effect sizes depended heavily on the control condition: 0.41 against no-treatment controls versus 0.14 against active controls. — [PMC11420571 (Australian SSI review)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11420571/)
- Umbrella review (Schleider et al., *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology*, 2025): 24 systematic reviews covering 415 unique trials and about 40,629 participants aged 5–90. 20 of 24 reviews (83%) reported significant positive effects on at least one outcome (anxiety, depression, externalizing, eating, substance use, treatment engagement or uptake). Across the 12 reviews that pooled SSIs against controls, SMD = −0.25 (I² = 43%). AMSTAR 2 flagged methodological concerns, including low rates of preregistration. The abstract does not report separate estimates for adults and youth. — [Northwestern news release](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2025/02/single-session-interventions-significantly-reduce-mental-health-issues-for-youth-and-adults); [IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2025/journal-article-abstracts/annurev-clinpsy-081423-025033/)
- Youth depression specifically: a meta-analysis of 15 RCTs found g = −0.12 (95% CI −0.23 to −0.01), with substantial heterogeneity and a wide prediction interval. This is cited in the 2026 megastudy. — [Megastudy PDF, Nature Human Behaviour 2026](https://www.schleiderlab.org/uploads/2/1/8/4/21847128/a_crowdsourced_megastudy_of_12_digital_single-session_interventions_for_depression_in_us_adults.pdf)
- Eating disorders, mixed ages (2026 meta-analysis, 22 studies): pooled g = −0.23 (95% CI −0.40 to −0.05). Self-guided and remotely delivered SSIs were effective. — [PubMed 41693024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41693024/)
- Adult problem drinking: single-session personalized normative feedback had g = 0.27 (95% CI 0.11–0.43), significantly less than extended e-self-help (g = 0.61). Here a single session helped but did less than multi-session help. — [PMC3221381](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3221381/)

**Adult trials (including disconfirming evidence)**
- 2026 crowdsourced megastudy (*Nature Human Behaviour*, DOI 10.1038/s41562-026-02415-6; preregistered RCT NCT06856668). N = 7,505 US adults with elevated depressive symptoms, recruited via Prolific and CloudResearch Connect and paid US$4.25. Arms: 11 crowdsourced 10-minute SSIs, a behavioural-activation SSI as active comparator, and an attention-matched control. 97.8% completed the session and 77.6% completed the 4-week follow-up. Nearly all SSIs improved outcomes immediately after the session (d ≤ 0.37). At 4 weeks only two significantly reduced depression: Interactive Cognitive Reappraisal (d = 0.15) and Mindful Attention Skills (d = 0.14). Neither survived correction for multiple comparisons. SSI recipients reported slightly lower readiness to change at 4 weeks (d = 0.05), which the authors flag as a possible mild iatrogenic effect ("the second published instance of an iatrogenic effect from an SSI"). Participants with no formal mental-health support in the past month improved more (d = −0.12). Stated limitations: 4-week follow-up only, all SSIs under 10 minutes, and minimal adverse-event monitoring. The authors write: "SSIs are not a replacement for psychotherapy, nor are they sufficient to produce durable behaviour change in most cases." They also note that "multiple well-powered trials of SSIs with adult samples have not found substantial effects on depression… or related outcomes such as anxiety." — [Megastudy PDF](https://www.schleiderlab.org/uploads/2/1/8/4/21847128/a_crowdsourced_megastudy_of_12_digital_single-session_interventions_for_depression_in_us_adults.pdf)
- Lorenzo-Luaces & Howard (2023, *JMIR*), COMET-SSI (CBT plus positive psychology, unguided): a preregistered, investigator-blinded RCT with N = 828 Prolific adults (409 vs 419 waitlist); 88% screened positive for depression or anxiety. Between-group differences were negligible at 2 and 8 weeks, including in more severe subgroups, despite power to detect small effects. The authors conclude: "Our results do not support the use of the COMET-SSI in adult Prolific participants." — [JMIR 2023;25:e45411](https://jmir.org/2023/1/e45411)
- A 2025 *Clinical Psychological Science* paper titled "Longer Single-Session Interventions May Not Be Better" reported two RCTs with adult online workers. I could not retrieve the results. — [Schleider lab publications](https://www.schleiderlab.org/publications.html)

**Project YES (youth, digital, self-guided)**
- National RCT (Schleider et al., 2022, *Nature Human Behaviour*) in 2,452 US adolescents aged 13–16 with elevated depressive symptoms during COVID-19. Two SSIs (Project Personality, ABC Project) beat a supportive control at 3 months on depression, hopelessness, anxiety and restrictive eating (ps < .001). Effects were similar across marginalized-identity subgroups. I took these figures from the lab summary and could not verify effect sizes in the primary paper. — [Schleider Lab Project YES page](https://schleiderlab.org/yes.html)
- In real-world dissemination in San Antonio, 894 of 1,801 youths who started (49.6%) completed the 30-minute SSI. — [JMIR Mental Health 2023;e43062](https://mental.jmir.org/2023/1/e43062)

**Human-delivered walk-in and appointment single-session therapy (SST)**
- Ontario walk-in counselling (Stalker et al., *Journal of Mental Health*, 2016; KW Counselling Services and Family Service Thames Valley). This was a mixed-methods comparison of existing service models, not an RCT; news coverage reports 532 participants aged 16+. On the GHQ, walk-in clients improved faster and were less distressed at 4 weeks than waitlist clients. By 10 weeks both groups had improved and were similar. The authors note that walk-in designs "are not conducive to randomized controlled trials." — [WLU Scholars record](https://scholars.wlu.ca/scwk_faculty/16); [CBC coverage](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/kitchener-waterloo/walk-in-therapy-clinics-more-effective-than-traditional-counselling-sessions-study-finds-1.3412408)
- Ontario cost-effectiveness (Lamsal et al., 2017): the QALY gain over 10 weeks was 0.0039 (not significant). Societal and payer costs were higher for walk-in. The conclusion was that walk-in SST "is not cost-effective compared to being on the waitlist for traditional counselling." The source thesis instead described walk-in as cost-saving but non-significant, so the two framings conflict. — [UWSpace thesis](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/8696?show=full); [J Mental Health 2017 record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09638237.2017.1340619)
- Qualitative companion study: walk-in outcomes depended on accessibility, how clients made sense of the service, and their motivation and ability to engage. — [WLU Scholars](https://scholars.wlu.ca/scwk_faculty/17)
- Kaiser Permanente (Talmon, Hoyt & Rosenbaum, Hayward CA, 1986–1990). Of 58 consecutive outpatients aged 8–80 offered SST, 34 (58.6%) chose to finish in one session even though more were available. More than 88% of one-session patients reported significant improvement in the presenting complaint and more than 65% reported "ripple" improvements. This was uncontrolled, self-reported follow-up from a single clinic, and these figures come from secondary summaries. — [Moshe Talmon (Wikipedia summary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moshe_Talmon); [Talmon 2020 retrospective, ANZJFT](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/australian-and-new-zealand-journal-of-family-therapy/article/when-less-is-more-lessons-from-25-years-of-attempting-to-maximize-the-effect-of-each-and-often-only-therapeutic-encounter/59E27588F12F194CF9672C1C8FF48613)
- Talmon's 2020 retrospective lists three conclusions from the Kaiser work: (1) single session is the most common length of psychotherapy, (2) the first session is potentially the most therapeutic, and (3) SST is the most cost-effective mode. These are the author's claims, not new data. — [ANZJFT 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/australian-and-new-zealand-journal-of-family-therapy/article/when-less-is-more-lessons-from-25-years-of-attempting-to-maximize-the-effect-of-each-and-often-only-therapeutic-encounter/59E27588F12F194CF9672C1C8FF48613)
- Australian child and adolescent services (Perkins, 2006, *Psychology and Psychotherapy*): solution-focused SST was offered to all clients at an urban CAMHS for 14 months. Parent and clinician ratings improved significantly 1 month after a single session. Perkins & Scarlett (2008) reported benefits maintained at 18 months, with some clients needing boosters. Abstracts describe a pre/post clinic design, though one later meta-analysis calls it a randomized trial, so the design is ambiguous. — [Perkins 2006](https://zendy.io/title/10.1348/147608305x60523); [Perkins & Scarlett 2008](https://zendy.io/title/10.1348/147608308x280995)
- Bouverie Centre (Melbourne): I found no published outcome evaluation of its single-session family consultation. Bouverie's own framework says it rests partly on "practice wisdom". O'Neill & Rottem (2012) describe a 12-month agency-wide implementation, but its outcomes were not available in the abstract. — [VCCMHW / Bouverie framework](https://vccmhw.vic.gov.au/resources/FROM-INDIVIDUAL-TO-FAMILIES-A-CLIENT-CENTRED-FRAMEWORK-FOR-INVOLVING-FAMILIES.html); [O'Neill & Rottem, ANZJFT](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/australian-and-new-zealand-journal-of-family-therapy/article/reflections-and-learning-from-an-agencywide-implementation-of-single-session-work-in-family-therapy/99B6AE6D1481BA79DF32B25F9788F9DE)
- Hymmen, Stalker & Cait (2013, *Journal of Mental Health*) reviewed the SST literature and found that most clients attending scheduled or walk-in SST rated it sufficient and helpful. The evidence was mostly satisfaction and uncontrolled data. — [J Mental Health 2013](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.3109/09638237.2012.670880)
- University walk-in clinic in British Columbia (UBC Okanagan, 2024, *PLOS ONE*): 693 first-appointment records. Post-session ratings averaged 88/100 for satisfaction and 83/100 for helpfulness. No pre/post symptom change was measured. — [PMC11142450](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11142450/)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports SSIs as a useful first or stand-alone touchpoint that delivers immediate relief, hope and agency. It does not support one session as a substitute for ongoing treatment for clinical depression in adults. Effects at 4–10 weeks tend to fade to the level of controls (megastudy; Ontario at 10 weeks).
- The strongest adult evidence comes from digital, self-guided SSIs with paid online participants, not from one-off sessions with a human therapist. Data on human-delivered SST in adults are mostly uncontrolled (Kaiser) or quasi-experimental (Ontario). An app selling human one-off sessions is therefore leaning on weaker evidence than the headline SSI meta-analyses.
- A possible iatrogenic signal (lower readiness to change after the initial boost fades) argues for framing a one-off session as a bridge with clear next steps, not as a fix.
- The evidence is best for youth anxiety and conduct problems. It is weakest for depression and substance use, and the adult-specific data are mixed.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests a one-off session with an unfamiliar therapist for someone already in ongoing therapy, such as a traveller or a client whose usual therapist is unavailable that day. This is the app's core original use case, and I found no direct evidence on it.
- I could not obtain the primary effect sizes from Project YES (2022) or the 2025 "Longer SSIs May Not Be Better" trials.
- I found no published Bouverie Centre outcome data.
- I could not access the Hymmen et al. (2013) full text to check its quality appraisal.

## 2. Attendance: the distribution of sessions, whether one session is the mode, and dropout rates

### Takeaway
In routine outpatient care the distribution of sessions is strongly right-skewed. Several sources report one session as the modal number in community settings. In US national data, about 28–34% of adult psychotherapy users stop after one or two visits. Meta-analytic dropout from adult psychotherapy averages about 20%, but varies from 0% to 74% depending on definition and setting.

### Cited Findings
- Talmon's Kaiser Permanente work (1986–1990) concluded that "single session is the most common length of psychotherapy." — [ANZJFT 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/australian-and-new-zealand-journal-of-family-therapy/article/when-less-is-more-lessons-from-25-years-of-attempting-to-maximize-the-effect-of-each-and-often-only-therapeutic-encounter/59E27588F12F194CF9672C1C8FF48613)
- A 2019 review reports that in community mental-health settings the most common number of sessions was one. It also reports that about 70% of early discontinuers stop after one or two sessions, citing Olfson et al. (2009). I saw this only as a search snippet because the page was CAPTCHA-blocked. — [PMC6768545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6768545)
- Barrett, Chua, Crits-Christoph et al. (2008, *Psychotherapy*) reviewed early withdrawal from mental-health treatment. I could not verify its "modal session = 1" wording directly. — [PMC2762228](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2762228)
- US national trends (Olfson et al., *JAMA Psychiatry*, 2024; household survey data, 6,415 adults with psychotherapy visits). The share stopping after one or two sessions fell from 34% (2018) to 28% (2021). The share attending more than 20 sessions rose from under 14% to over 17%. About 40% of adult psychotherapy users in 2021 had at least one teletherapy session. — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/mental-health/changing-trends-seen-in-psychotherapy-in-outpatient-mental-health-care); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/12/241204113537.htm)
- Cross-national data (Flückiger, Wampold, Delgadillo, Rubel, Vîslă & Lutz, 2020, *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics*): 20 naturalistic routine-outcome-monitoring samples from 8 countries. Mean sessions per sample ranged from 2.86 to 45.1. The median of the sample means was 8.21 and the mean was 12.90. Most samples did not exceed 16 sessions, but German samples ran much longer. Country policy (for example UK session caps versus unlimited coverage in Switzerland) strongly shapes session counts. — [Karger](https://karger.com/pps/article/89/5/333/283207/Is-There-an-Evidence-Based-Number-of-Sessions-in)
- Swift & Greenberg (2012, *JCCP*) meta-analysed dropout across 669 studies and about 84,000 adult clients. The weighted average was 19.7%, with a range of 0–74%. Few predictors were consistent, and those were small: younger age, lower education, eating-disorder or personality-disorder diagnosis. — [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2015/04/clients); [Swift & Greenberg PDF](https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/37._dropout_meta_analysis.pdf); [GoodTherapy summary](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/why-clients-drop-out-therapy-0809123)
- Swift et al. (2017, *Psychotherapy*) found that clients assigned to pharmacotherapy were 1.20 times as likely to drop out as those assigned to psychotherapy. — [Psychiatric News Alert](https://alert.psychnews.org/2017/03/refusal-dropout-rates-differ-between.html?m=0)
- A blog post (low-quality secondary source) reports an analysis of more than 460,000 patients' records in which 77% of psychotherapies ended after 24 sessions or fewer. — [Tim Kaiser blog](https://longform.social/post/did:plc:lt5la54j5jz3uewon47gbeeh/3lxhrs2wjrs2y)

### Inferences
- A large share of real-world "therapy episodes" are already de facto single sessions. So designing every first session to stand alone (a goal, something actionable, a next step) is evidence-aligned, whatever the app's model.
- Because many people who stop after one session report being satisfied (Kaiser), single attendance should not automatically be counted as failed dropout. Equally, the 2–4 week decay in SSI effects means follow-up contact matters.
- Session-count norms are partly set by health-system policy, so benchmarks will differ by market. US, UK and Germany are not comparable.

### Gaps
- I found no primary dataset giving the full distribution of US session counts (for example the share attending exactly one session). The Olfson 2024 tables likely contain it, but I could not access them.
- Data on first-appointment no-show rates were not retrieved.

## 3. Matching: alliance, performance-based matching, preferences, racial/ethnic and gender matching, therapist effects, and platform claims

### Takeaway
The alliance robustly predicts outcome (r ≈ .28). The only RCT of a matching method, Constantino et al. (2021), matched on therapists' measured outcome strengths by problem domain, not on demographics or "vibe", and found large effects (d = 0.50–0.75). Accommodating client preferences reduces dropout (OR 1.79) and modestly improves outcomes (d = 0.28). Racial/ethnic matching is strongly preferred (d = 0.63) and improves how clients perceive therapy (d ≈ 0.32), but has almost no effect on outcomes (d = 0.09). Evidence on gender matching is null or inconsistent. Therapists account for about 5% of outcome variance (range 0.2–29%). Platform claims of algorithmic matching rest on vendor statistics and one industry-authored retrospective study, with no independent controlled evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Alliance**
- Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold & Horvath (2018, *Psychotherapy*; APA Division 29 task force): 295 independent studies with more than 30,000 patients (1978–2017). Alliance–outcome r = .278 (95% CI .256–.299), equivalent to d = .579. Internet-based therapy gave a similar r = .275 (k = 23). The association was consistent across rater perspective, measures, treatment approaches, patient characteristics and countries. — [Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy PDF](https://www.societyforpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Fluckiger-et-al-2018.pdf); [Division 29 summary](https://www.societyforpsychotherapy.org/the-alliance-in-adult-psychotherapy/)

**Performance-based matching (the only RCT)**
- Constantino et al. (2021, *JAMA Psychiatry* 78(9):960–969; NCT02990000): a double-blind RCT (patients and therapists unaware of match status) in 6 community clinics in Cleveland, Ohio, 2017–2019. 1,329 screened, 288 randomized, 218 analyzed (modified ITT), 48 therapists. Each therapist was classified as effective, neutral or ineffective in each of 12 Treatment Outcome Package domains, based on at least 15 historical cases. Patients were matched to therapists strong in their most elevated domains; the comparison was assignment as usual (availability or location). Weekly-change effects favouring matching were d = 0.75 for general impairment, d = 0.50 for global distress (SCL-10), and d = 0.60 for domain-specific impairment. No adverse events were reported. A press release says effects were larger for more severe patients and for racial or ethnic minority patients. — [Abstract via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22Effect%20of%20Matching%20Therapists%20to%20Patients%20vs%20Assignment%20as%20Usual%22&resultType=core&format=json); [UMass news](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/evidence-based-patient-psychotherapist-matching-improves-mental-health-care)
- A secondary analysis (Coyne et al., 2024) found that the match effect on global distress was significantly moderated only by how much effectiveness varied across a therapist's domains. Matching helps most when therapists have clear strengths and weaknesses. — [Adm Policy Ment Health 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10488-024-01360-8)
- In the UK (IAPT; Delgadillo, Rubel & Barkham, 2020, *JCCP*), machine learning on 2,425 patients and 68 therapists found 17 classes of patient–therapist matches with differential effectiveness on PHQ-9 reliable improvement. This was retrospective and not prospectively tested. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/160792)

**Client preferences**
- Swift, Callahan, Cooper & Parkin (2018, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*): 53 studies with more than 16,000 clients. Accommodating preferences was associated with fewer dropouts (OR = 1.79) and better outcomes (d = 0.28). The effect was moderated by study design, outcome timing and type, and diagnosis, but not by preference type, age, gender, ethnicity or education. — [Library record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2086257428)

**Racial/ethnic matching**
- Cabral & Smith (2011, *Journal of Counseling Psychology*): preference for a same-race or same-ethnicity therapist d = 0.63 (52 studies); perceptions of the therapist d ≈ 0.32–0.33; outcomes d = 0.09 (53 studies), described as "almost no benefit". African American clients showed the largest effects on all three. Heterogeneity was high. — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/80); [APA PDF](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jcoup/pdf/10.1037/a0025266~racialethnic-matching-of-clients-and-therapists-in-mental)

**Gender matching**
- A meta-analysis by Bowman et al. (64 studies, depressed outpatients), as summarized by Cottone (2002), found that client gender, therapist gender and gender matching were not significantly related to outcome. — [Cottone 2002 PDF](https://cdn3.psychologytoday.com/assets/cottone_2002_psychotherapy_0.pdf)
- Schmalbach et al. (2022, *BMC Psychology*; Germany, N = 1,212) found only a trend toward better outcomes in same-gender dyads (mainly female–female, in psychodynamic therapy). The authors conclude the benefit "is not clear." — [PMC8895875](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8895875/)

**Therapist effects**
- Johns, Barkham, Kellett & Saxon (2019, *Clinical Psychology Review*): 20 studies (3 RCTs, 17 practice-based). Therapists explained about 5% of outcome variance on average, with a range of about 0.2–29% (some secondary sources say 30%). Effects were larger with more severe patients. The authors suggest reporting by setting. Crits-Christoph et al. (1991) earlier estimated 8.6%. — [White Rose author copy](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/138182/1/Johns%20et%20al.%20A%20systematic%20review%20of%20therapist%20effects%20-%20author%27s%20copy%20311018.pdf)

**Platform and algorithmic matching claims**
- Grow Therapy (Forman-Hoffman et al., 2026, *JMIR Formative Research*) ran a retrospective, uncontrolled analysis of 159,448 patients with depressive symptoms and 167,356 with anxiety symptoms. About 35% chose a provider specialty and about 5% chose a provider identity. Choosing a specialty was associated with better outcomes, and choosing an identity with more engagement. Of patients with 2+ visits, 58.9% met the PHQ-9 minimal clinically important difference (MCID) and 63% met the GAD-7 MCID. All six authors are Grow Therapy staff, and the authors call the study "exploratory, real-world, uncontrolled." — [JMIR Form Res 2026;10:e81121](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e81121)
- BetterHelp markets a "93% successful match" rate and "72% symptom reduction". These are vendor figures with no independent methodology published. — [BetterHelp match commitment page](https://www.betterhelp.com/therapist-match-commitment/)
- I found no independent RCT or peer-reviewed evaluation of BetterHelp, Talkspace or similar platforms' matching algorithms. Talkspace's research page lists studies, but they are not matching trials. — [Talkspace research](https://talkspace.com/research)

### Inferences
- The only experimentally supported matching approach works by routing each client to a therapist who has measured success with that client's specific problem. That requires collecting outcomes on therapists, client by client and domain by domain. A "Tinder-like" swipe on photos, bios or demographics has no outcome evidence. At best it could act through preference accommodation, which mainly reduces dropout. For a one-off-session product, though, dropout matters less than first-session fit.
- Identity matching (race/ethnicity, possibly gender) is worth offering because clients want it (d = 0.63) and it improves perceived credibility. It should not be marketed as improving clinical outcomes.
- Asking "what do you need today?" is consistent with preference-accommodation evidence and could be the input for domain-based matching (the TOP domains in Constantino). In a one-off context, therapists would need per-domain outcome data the app has collected itself, which creates a cold-start problem; Constantino required at least 15 prior cases per therapist.
- Because therapist effects are modest on average (about 5%) but large at the extremes, avoiding consistently ineffective therapists may add more value than fine-grained matching.

### Gaps
- I found no evidence on matching for a single session. All matching studies concern multi-session episodes.
- I found no RCT of client-chooses-therapist ("browse") versus assigned matching.
- I did not retrieve the 2018 Division 29 updates on cultural adaptation or the 2019 Norcross & Lambert compendium effect sizes beyond alliance and preferences.
- I could not verify the size of the subgroup effects in the Constantino press release (severity, minority status).

## 4. Continuity of care: changing therapists, relational continuity, temporary coverage, and split treatment

### Takeaway
In the one RCT found, switching therapists after non-response neither helped nor harmed outcomes for depressed outpatients. Observational evidence links poor relational continuity to higher mortality and suicide risk in serious mental illness and after discharge, but certainty is low or very low. I found no direct evidence on temporary coverage or one-off substitute sessions for people in ongoing therapy.

### Cited Findings
- Miggiels et al. (2026, *Journal of Affective Disorders* 394:120556; Netherlands, Dimence specialty mental-health care, 4 sites). 163 non-responders to 16 sessions of CBT or short-term psychodynamic supportive therapy were randomized 1:1:1 to continue with the same therapist, switch therapist, or switch therapist and method, with 16 more sessions each. There were no significant differences in symptom reduction, response or remission. Continuing therapy produced modest improvement (mean IDS-SR change −5.23, 95% CI −7.27 to −3.12). The authors conclude there is "no one-size-fits-all switching strategy." Sources disagree on the citation year (2025 vs 2026). — [HCPLive summary](https://www.hcplive.com/view/switching-therapists-psychotherapy-offers-no-advantage-mdd)
- Engström et al. (2023, *BMC Psychiatry*) systematically reviewed relational continuity in serious mental illness. Higher relational continuity "may prevent premature deaths and suicide," but certainty of evidence was low or very low for all outcomes, and exposure measures were inconsistent. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/676f444fee6140b49aec5b336229f2a8)
- Arnon, Shahar & Brunstein Klomek (2024, *Frontiers in Public Health*), a narrative review: poor continuity of care is associated with readmission, attempted suicide and suicide death. One cited study (Burgess et al., 2000) estimated that about 23% of suicides were partly attributable to continuity problems such as abrupt termination of services or loss of a case manager. King et al. studied "changes in key personnel" among people who died by suicide within a year of discharge. About 45% of people who died by suicide had seen primary care in the prior month. Most continuity studies are quasi-experimental. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1266717/full)
- Therapist effects are about 5% of outcome variance on average (Johns et al., 2019), so on average the identity of the therapist matters only modestly to outcome. — [White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/138182/1/Johns%20et%20al.%20A%20systematic%20review%20of%20therapist%20effects%20-%20author%27s%20copy%20311018.pdf)

### Inferences
- For stable outpatients, an occasional session with a different clinician is unlikely to harm outcomes, based on indirect evidence from the switching RCT and modest average therapist effects. That trial, however, involved full 16-session courses, not single substitute visits.
- The risk from fragmentation is concentrated in high-risk groups: serious mental illness, people recently discharged, and people with suicidal ideation. For them, losing a known clinician and poor handoffs are linked to suicide. A "therapist unavailable today" product should screen these clients and route them, with consent, back to or alongside their primary clinician, using information-sharing and a written handoff.
- A substitute session that conflicts with the primary therapist's formulation could in principle confuse or undermine treatment. I found no data on this.

### Gaps
- I found no studies on temporary or vacation coverage sessions, on split or concurrent psychotherapy with two therapists, or on harms from fragmented outpatient psychotherapy specifically. The search budget ran out before I could pursue this.
- I found no data on how often people in ongoing therapy seek an outside one-off session, or what happens when they do.

## 5. Telehealth: video, phone and text compared with in-person

### Takeaway
Across multiple meta-analyses, video and telephone therapy are roughly equivalent to in-person therapy on symptom outcomes, with differences near zero (g ≈ 0.04–0.06). The alliance is similar or slightly lower by video. A large RCT run on Talkspace found message-based therapy comparable to weekly video for mild-to-moderate depression, but it excluded higher-risk patients and was industry-linked. Most of the evidence concerns CBT for depression and anxiety.

### Cited Findings
- Norwood et al. (2018, *Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy*), a non-inferiority meta-analysis: symptom reduction by video was non-inferior to face-to-face (SMD = −0.03, 95% CI −0.45 to 0.40; 12 studies, 6 with face-to-face controls). Working alliance by video was lower, and its CI crossed the non-inferiority margin. — [Infona record](https://www.infona.pl/resource/bwmeta1.element.wiley-cpp-v-25-i-6-cpp2315)
- A 2023 *Journal of Telemedicine and Telecare* meta-analysis (18 publications) found no significant difference in patient-rated alliance between video and in-person therapy (SMD = −0.09, 95% CI −0.26 to 0.07). — [PubMed 36974478](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36974478/)
- A 2022 meta-analysis of depression RCTs found video psychotherapy roughly equal to in-person (g = 0.04). — [PubMed 35007437](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35007437/)
- Hagi et al. (2023, *British Journal of Psychiatry*), a telepsychiatry meta-analysis of RCTs: one source reports 32 studies (n = 3,592) across 11 disorders, another 26 studies in the main analysis. There was no overall difference from face-to-face care. Telepsychiatry showed a small borderline advantage in a depression subgroup (6 studies; SMD −0.325, 95% CI −0.640 to −0.011). The study counts differ between secondary sources. — [PubMed 37655816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37655816/)
- Telephone CBT (Altieri et al., 2023, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*; 33 studies) did not differ from face-to-face CBT for depression (g = 0.06, p = .466). — [Altieri et al. PDF](https://ricerca.unich.it/retrieve/3ebdabd7-9ee8-4e3e-9fc4-5e95b1e28ca6/Altieri_M_etal_JCP_2023%28The%20efficacy%20of%20telephone%e2%80%90delivered%20CBT%29.pdf)
- A 2024 meta-analysis of 6 RCTs comparing remote and in-person therapy found depression SMD −0.10 (95% CI −0.57 to 0.37; I² = 77%) and anxiety SMD −0.06 (95% CI −0.34 to 0.21). — [Bohrium index](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/39304185)
- A 2023 *Psychotherapy Research* cohort study (479 teletherapy vs 479 in-person clients) found non-inferior alliance and outcomes. — [IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=1566903)
- Message-based therapy (University of Washington and Talkspace, *JAMA Network Open*, 30 Oct 2025; NIMH R44 MH124334). An RCT of 850 adults with mild-to-moderate depression compared asynchronous messaging with weekly video for 12 weeks; non-responders at 6 weeks were re-randomized to a combination. Improvements in depression and social functioning were comparable, and the video group was slightly more likely to disengage early. Higher-risk patients, including those with suicidal thoughts, were excluded. One author was a Talkspace employee. The trial was not designed to test equivalence. — [UW Medicine newsroom](https://newsroom.uw.edu/news-releases/texts-as-effective-as-live-video-for-depression-therapy); [news coverage](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/10/31/study-finds-evidence-that-text-based-therapy-eases-depression)
- The alliance–outcome link holds for internet-based therapy (r = .275, k = 23). — [Flückiger et al. 2018](https://www.societyforpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Fluckiger-et-al-2018.pdf)
- About 40% of US adult psychotherapy users had at least one teletherapy session in 2021. — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/12/241204113537.htm)

### Inferences
- Remote delivery of a one-off session is not itself an evidence problem; video and phone are well supported for common mental disorders. The weak points are risk management at a distance and the exclusion of high-risk patients from the trials.
- Text-only one-off sessions have little direct support. The Talkspace trial involved 12 weeks of ongoing messaging, not a single exchange.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of single-session therapy delivered by telehealth with a human clinician.
- I found no evidence on telehealth for severe mental illness or acute risk in this search.
- I did not retrieve exact non-inferiority margins or pooled effects for text-based therapy beyond the single RCT.

## 6. Safety: how often first-contact or same-day seekers are in crisis, brief suicide interventions, and the risks of seeing a clinician with no history

### Takeaway
Elevated suicide risk is common enough at first contact that every session needs structured screening. One walk-in clinic flagged suicidality in roughly 10–13% of first appointments. PHQ-9 item 9 stratifies one-year attempt risk from 0.4% to 4%. Safety-planning-type interventions reduce subsequent suicidal behaviour (RR 0.57, NNT 16) but do not reduce ideation. The best-known study bundled a safety plan with follow-up phone calls and was non-randomized. Brief contact interventions on their own have not reduced repeat self-harm or suicide. Poor continuity is linked to suicide in high-risk groups.

### Cited Findings

**How often risk presents**
- At the UBC Okanagan walk-in clinic (Canada; 693 first-appointment records), CAT-MH flagged suicidality in 29 clients before COVID and 54 during it. By my calculation that is about 10% and 13% of the two periods (n ≈ 285 and 408); the authors did not report these percentages. The flag is binary and does not separate ideation, intent and plan. Session limits could be relaxed when suicidal ideation or imminent risk was present. — [PMC11142450](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11142450/)
- In single-session and walk-in models, risk is assessed as in appointment-based care, followed by safety planning and referral to the emergency department when risk is serious (Slive & Bobele). — [SAFTI PDF](https://safti.squarespace.com/s/JST-doubts-re-sst.pdf)
- SuicideLine Victoria (Australia) runs a risk assessment at the start of every single session and adds or substitutes other support when risk is higher. — [SuicideLine Victoria](https://suicideline.org.au/health-professionals/how-our-counselling-services-work/)
- A review notes that one walk-in service collected mood and risk ratings before sessions but did not report the risk data. — [Hymmen et al. 2013](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.3109/09638237.2012.670880)

**Screening signal**
- Simon et al. (2013, *Psychiatric Services*; a US integrated health system, 2007–2011) linked 84,418 outpatients aged 13+ and 207,265 PHQ-9s to outcomes, recording 709 attempts and 46 suicide deaths. One-year attempt risk rose from 0.4% ("not at all" on item 9) to 4% ("nearly every day"). One-year suicide-death risk rose from 0.03% to 0.3%. Item 9 remained predictive after adjusting for depression severity and treatment history. Risk built up over days and kept growing for months. — [Psychiatric Services abstract](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ps.201200587)
- A letter to the editor (disconfirming) noted that 98% of positive item-9 responses were followed by no attempt within 90 days, and that more than half of the 90-day periods containing an attempt followed a "not at all" response. Item 9 is therefore a poor tool for predicting risk in an individual. — [Psychiatric Services letter](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.70704)

**Brief and single-contact interventions**
- Nuij et al. (2021, *British Journal of Psychiatry*), a meta-analysis of safety-planning-type interventions: 6 studies with 3,536 participants. Relative risk of suicidal behaviour was 0.570 (95% CI 0.408–0.795) and NNT = 16. There was no significant effect on suicidal ideation. A published letter argued the authors were too optimistic given heterogeneity. — [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/238354)
- Stanley, Brown et al. (2018, *JAMA Psychiatry* 75(9):894–900) ran a cohort comparison in 9 VA emergency departments (5 intervention, 4 control; n = 1,186 vs 454; about 88% men). The Safety Planning Intervention, including lethal-means counselling, plus at least 2 follow-up phone calls was associated with 3.03% vs 5.29% suicidal behaviour over 6 months (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.95, about 45% lower odds). It roughly doubled the odds of attending outpatient mental-health care (OR 2.06). The design was non-randomized, and the intervention was not a single contact. — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/psychiatry/20180711/intervention-with-followup-reduces-suicidal-behavior-after-ed-discharge)
- Milner et al. (2015, *British Journal of Psychiatry*) meta-analysed brief contact interventions (postcards, calls, crisis cards): 14 RCTs, 12 pooled. Effects were not significant for subsequent self-harm or suicide attempt (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.74–1.04) or for suicide (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.24–1.38). The number of repetitions per person fell (IRR 0.66), but only 3 studies contributed. The authors concluded these interventions "cannot yet be recommended for widespread clinical implementation." Many studies were old or at high risk of bias. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/00C9876D2C0D667BFAD3995A4C41FFD5/core-reader); [Suicideinfo summary](https://www.suicideinfo.ca/?p=100327)

**Risks of fragmentation and of no history**
- About 23% of suicides were partly attributed to continuity problems such as abrupt termination or loss of a case manager (Burgess et al., 2000, cited in a review). Recommended practice is follow-up contact within 7 days of an ED visit or discharge. — [Arnon et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1266717/full)
- Evidence on relational continuity and suicide in serious mental illness is low or very low certainty. — [Engström et al. 2023](https://doaj.org/article/676f444fee6140b49aec5b336229f2a8)
- The 2026 adult SSI megastudy had "minimal adverse event monitoring" and found a small drop in readiness to change, which may be iatrogenic. — [Megastudy PDF](https://www.schleiderlab.org/uploads/2/1/8/4/21847128/a_crowdsourced_megastudy_of_12_digital_single-session_interventions_for_depression_in_us_adults.pdf)
- The large text-versus-video RCT excluded people with suicidal thoughts, so platform evidence does not cover high-risk users. — [news coverage](https://www.aol.com/articles/goodbye-zoom-therapy-simple-text-173548431.html)

### Inferences
- A one-off-session app needs a mandatory structured risk screen before or at the start of every session (for example PHQ-9 item 9 or a brief Columbia-type screen). It also needs a clinician protocol for a Stanley–Brown safety plan within the session, a known local emergency pathway for the client's actual location (critical for travellers), and an automatic follow-up contact within about 7 days for anyone screening positive. The evidence for safety planning is strongest when it is paired with follow-up, which a strictly "one-off" model omits.
- A clinician with no history lacks baseline risk information: prior attempts, the current treating clinician, medications. With consent, collecting the regular therapist's contact and a brief summary would mitigate this. I found no direct studies quantifying this risk.
- Excluding acute-risk clients and routing them to crisis services is consistent with how the main platform trials were run, but it means the app should not market itself for crisis use.

### Gaps
- I found no study reporting what proportion of same-day or on-demand teletherapy users present in acute crisis.
- I found no direct evidence on adverse events or missed risk when a clinician has no history with the client.
- I did not retrieve evidence on cross-jurisdiction emergency response for travellers (licensure, local 911/112 dispatch from telehealth). This is likely a regulatory question for another researcher.
- I did not retrieve psychometric data on the C-SSRS screener.

## 7. Outcome measurement: brief, validated measures for a one-off-session app

### Takeaway
Routine outcome monitoring with feedback produces small but reliable gains (d ≈ 0.15, dropout OR 1.19). Evidence for PCOMS, which uses the ORS and SRS, is contested: overall g = 0.27, but non-significant in psychiatric settings (g = 0.10) and possibly inflated by researcher allegiance and ORS-only outcomes. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are well-validated screens with standard cutoffs of 10 and are used as MCID-based outcomes by platforms. For a single session, the SSI literature uses immediate proximal measures (hopelessness, agency) plus a 2–4 week follow-up, because immediate gains decay.

### Cited Findings
- de Jong et al. (2021, *Clinical Psychology Review* 85:102002), a multilevel meta-analysis of feedback: 58 studies, 110 effect sizes, 21,699 patients. Symptom-outcome effect d = 0.15 overall and d = 0.17 for not-on-track cases. Feedback produced a small favourable effect on dropout (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.03–1.38). — [VU record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/using-progress-feedback-to-improve-outcomes-and-reduce-drop-out-t/); [PMC11703940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11703940)
- Østergård et al. (2018/2020, *Psychotherapy Research*) meta-analysed PCOMS (ORS/SRS): 18 studies (14 RCTs) with 2,910 participants. Overall g = 0.27 with substantial heterogeneity. The effect was not significant in psychiatric settings (g = 0.10) and was g = 0.45 in counselling settings, where the authors suspect allegiance bias and inflation from using the ORS as the only outcome. Duncan & Sparks rebutted the analysis, and Østergård & Hougaard replied that ORS-based outcomes may be inflated by social desirability. — [PubMed 30213240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30213240/); [Duncan & Sparks reply](https://biomed.reviewer.ly/app/publication/1578a80e-53dd-4f6b-aab2-993b7b99f249)
- A separate PCOMS meta-analysis that excluded ORS-only well-being outcomes found no evidence that PCOMS affected sessions attended or client well-being. — [x-mol record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1342584203739406336)
- The majority of PCOMS studies were conducted by PCOMS-affiliated researchers. — [Psychotherapy Research 2021](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/10503307.2021.2015637)
- GAD-7 at a cutoff of ≥10 had 89% sensitivity and 82% specificity for generalized anxiety disorder against a structured interview (Spitzer et al., 2006). A meta-analysis reportedly favoured a cutoff of 8 (0.83 and 0.84). The frequently cited MCID of about 4 points comes from Toussaint et al. (2020), which I saw only via a secondary source. — [CASRAI GAD-7 guide](https://www.casrai.org/guides/gad-7)
- PHQ-9 at a cutoff of ≥10 had 88% sensitivity and 88% specificity (Kroenke et al., 2001), taken from a secondary comparison table. The PHQ-9 MCID of about 5 points appeared only on a commercial site and needs primary verification. — [UCL BMJ Open 2023 PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10177858/1/e076193.full.pdf); [Twofold (commercial)](https://www.trytwofold.com/blog/phq9-gad7-progress-notes)
- Validity varies by population; for example, a GAD-7 cutoff of ≥9 in Lithuanian students gave 73% sensitivity and 70% specificity. — [Lithuanian validation](https://www.lituanistika.lt/content/44495)
- Platform precedent: Grow Therapy reported the share of patients meeting PHQ-9 and GAD-7 MCID (58.9% and 63%) as its outcome metric. — [JMIR Form Res 2026](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e81121)
- SSI trials use proximal session-level outcomes. Project YES measured hopelessness, self-hate, perceived control and agency; the megastudy measured hopelessness, agency, change expectancies and readiness to change, plus PHQ-9 at 4 weeks. — [JMIR Mental Health 2020](https://mental.jmir.org/2020/6/e20513/PDF); [Megastudy PDF](https://www.schleiderlab.org/uploads/2/1/8/4/21847128/a_crowdsourced_megastudy_of_12_digital_single-session_interventions_for_depression_in_us_adults.pdf)
- Ontario walk-in SST used the General Health Questionnaire at baseline, 4 weeks and 10 weeks. — [WLU / core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/143689828.pdf)
- PHQ-9 item 9 doubles as a risk-stratification item. — [Simon et al. 2013](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ps.201200587)

### Inferences
- A defensible minimum stack for one-off sessions would have four parts:
  1. Before the session: PHQ-9 (or PHQ-2 plus item 9) and GAD-7 for severity and risk triage, plus a "what do you need today" goal.
  2. After the session: an ultra-brief session-rating or alliance item (SRS-type) and a proximal measure such as hopelessness or agency, or a goal-progress rating.
  3. Follow-up: an automated PHQ-9 and GAD-7 at 2–4 weeks, since SSI effects decay.
  4. Matching data: outcomes logged per therapist per problem domain, so performance-based matching (Constantino) becomes possible over time.
- Do not rely on the ORS alone as the outcome measure, given evidence of inflation and allegiance bias. Pair it with an independent standardized measure.
- For a one-off model, single-session change scores are noisy and subject to regression to the mean. Platform-level claims would need a comparison group to be credible.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve psychometric data for the ORS or SRS (4-item visual analogue scales: reliability, validity, clinical cutoffs) because the search budget ran out.
- I did not retrieve data on the CORE-10, the Session Evaluation Questionnaire, Goal-Based Outcomes, or the PHQ-4.
- The PHQ-9 MCID and the primary PHQ-9 and GAD-7 validation statistics should be checked against Kroenke et al. (2001), Spitzer et al. (2006) and Toussaint et al. (2020).
- I found no validated measure designed specifically for single-session outcomes. The SSI field uses ad hoc proximal measures.
